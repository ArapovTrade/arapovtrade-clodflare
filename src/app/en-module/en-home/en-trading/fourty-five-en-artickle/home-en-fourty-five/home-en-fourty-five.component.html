How to start learning to trade

If you are new to trading, follow this study plan:

  • Read the chapters in order. Each stage uses concepts and rules introduced earlier.

Reading a bar's prices

On a standard chart, the data row shows the selected bar's open, high, low and close. Some values continue to change while the bar is still forming.

  • Open: the first price recorded for the bar.
  • High: the highest price recorded during its period.
  • Low: the lowest price recorded during its period.
  • Close: the final price for a completed bar, or the latest price while it is forming.

In the screenshot, the open is 76,342, the high is 77,904, the low is 75,705 and the latest price is 75,762 USDT per Bitcoin. These values describe the daily bar. They do not tell you whether the high or the low came first.

Bars and candlesticks

Standard OHLC bars and standard Japanese candlesticks display the same four prices for a period. Their shapes differ. Synthetic chart types may calculate different values, so use ordinary bars or candlesticks for the course exercises.

An OHLC bar has a left-hand tick for the open, a right-hand tick for the close, and a vertical line from the low to the high. A candlestick's body runs between the open and close; its wicks extend to the extremes. Colors depend on your chart settings.

The course illustrations use bars. You can use standard candlesticks for the same analysis because they represent the same four prices.

Volume indicator

The volume panel summarizes trading activity during each bar. For the illustrated crypto pair, check whether the indicator reports coins traded or turnover in the quoted currency. Neither measure is a count of individual trades. The data belongs to the selected exchange, not the entire Bitcoin market. Later chapters compare this activity with the price movement it produced.

Using different timeframes

A timeframe determines how much time one bar covers. A daily chart and a one-minute chart show different levels of detail, and their local trends can point in different directions. Note the timeframe before you mark the chart.

Use the two views for different tasks:

  • A higher timeframe, such as the daily chart, shows the broader structure and price zones. It provides context for the trade, but does not replace an entry condition on your working chart.
  • A lower timeframe, such as 15 minutes, shows more detail. Use it to check the entry and stop against your rules, while keeping the broader context in view.

The course examples use a 15-minute chart. Practice on a different timeframe requires its own assessment of trading costs and results. A recognizable chart pattern does not establish that a method performs equally well on every timeframe.

Bullish bars

A bar has four price fields to read:

  • O (Open): the opening price.
  • C (Close): the closing price.
  • H (High): the highest price during the period.
  • L (Low): the lowest price during the period.

A bullish bar closes above its opening price. It may be colored green, depending on the chart settings. The higher close tells you that price rose during that period. It does not tell you that there were more buyers than sellers: every completed trade has both a buyer and a seller.

Bearish bars

A bearish bar closes below its opening price. The example uses red, but the color can be changed. This is a description of the price movement, not a count of buyers and sellers.

Compare the open, close and extremes across successive bars to describe the movement and mark levels. One bar alone does not provide a complete trade setup.

From individual bars to market structure

Price waves are movements made up of sequences of bars. Within them, we identify impulses, pullbacks, and local highs and lows. These are the features used to describe market structure.

A wave can contain bars moving in both directions. Mark its boundaries using the relevant highs and lows, rather than requiring every bar to point the same way. The sequence of impulses and pullbacks helps you distinguish a trend from a range.

The illustrated sequence alternates between a directional move and a pullback. Sometimes price continues without the pullback your plan requires. In that case, there may be no planned entry.

Trading basics: spread, lot size, long and short

Spread and commission

Before placing a practice trade, check its costs, the units used for its size, and its direction. These determine what you are buying or selling and how the position's result is calculated.

A newly opened position may show a loss because of the spread and commission. Check the actual entry price and how the platform values an immediate exit.

The spread is the difference between the best available bid and ask. The ask is the quoted selling price available to a buyer; the bid is the quoted buying price available to a seller. Buying and immediately selling at those two prices produces a loss equal to the price difference, before any separate fees. The spread is not necessarily charged as a separate debit.

Suppose EUR/USD is available to buy at 1.0850 and sell at 1.0848. The spread is 0.0002, or two pips when one pip equals 0.0001. The dollar amount of that difference depends on the quantity traded. Use the prices that apply to the actual order, rather than treating a chart's last traded price as both the buy and sell price.

Commission is a separate trading fee. It may be fixed or depend on the quantity traded. Holding a position can also create financing costs. Before placing orders, check which costs are included in the spread and which are charged separately.

What is a lot?

A lot is a defined unit of trading quantity. A platform may ask for lots, contracts, shares or coins. Check both the quantity field and the product's specification before entering a number.

A common Forex specification defines a standard lot as 100,000 units of the base currency. Smaller sizes are often described as follows: Check whether your provider accepts those sizes and what increment it allows. These conventions do not mean that every instrument has the same lot size.

  • One standard lot: 100,000 units.
  • One mini lot, or 0.1 standard lot: 10,000 units.
  • One micro lot, or 0.01 standard lot: 1,000 units.

Exchange-traded futures use whole contracts. Their point value and contract size come from the individual contract specification. You cannot assume that a fraction accepted for a Forex lot also works for a futures contract. A calculated quantity of 0.233 of an indivisible exchange-traded contract cannot be opened as that quantity.

Even on a demo account, choose the quantity from your predetermined risk allowance. If the minimum available size is too large for that allowance, skip the trade or consider a suitable smaller instrument. Do not move the stop merely to make a larger position fit.

Long and short positions

A short position aims to benefit from a falling price. Compare it with buying an asset for a long position.

A long position benefits from a rising price. Buying at one price and selling at a higher price creates a positive price difference before costs. A fall in price produces a loss for the same position.

The opening mechanism of a short position depends on the product. A conventional stock short involves borrowing shares, selling them, then buying shares to return to the lender. A short futures position or CFD follows the contract’s rules; borrowing the underlying asset is not a necessary step in both products. A CFD is a contract for the difference between the opening and closing values.

For an illustrative EUR/USD short, an entry at 1.0850 and an exit at 1.0800 represent a favorable move of 50 standard pips before costs. The money earned depends on the position size and product specification. Those prices alone do not establish how any currency was borrowed.

In the course's basic system, I look for purchases within an upward structure and sales within a downward structure. Direction alone is insufficient: the level, return to the zone and continuation conditions still have to be checked.

Long and short describe the position's direction. An order tells the platform what action to take.

Types of trading orders

An order is an instruction to buy or sell a stated quantity. Start by separating the price at which execution is permitted from any condition that activates the instruction. The available order types depend on the product and trading venue.

Market orders

A market order seeks execution against available orders on the opposite side. It does not fix the price for the entire quantity. Partial fills, delays and trading restrictions can affect the result.

  • Buy: purchase at the available selling prices.
  • Sell: sell at the available buying prices.

Market orders prioritize execution over a specified price. Slippage is the difference between the price you expected and the actual execution price. An order may need to trade at several prices when insufficient quantity is available at the first one.

Limit and stop orders

A limit order sets a price boundary. It can execute immediately if available opposite-side prices already satisfy that boundary. A stop order waits for its activation condition. Reaching a chart level does not, by itself, guarantee that the requested quantity will be filled.

Limit:

  • Buy Limit sets the highest permitted purchase price. It is often placed below the market to wait for a decline. It can also fill immediately if an available ask is no higher than the limit.
  • Sell Limit sets the lowest permitted selling price. It can wait above the market for a rise, or fill immediately if the available bid satisfies the limit.

Stop:

  • Buy Stop activates a purchase when its specified condition is met, commonly above the current market. For a stop-market order, the final purchase price can differ from the trigger.
  • Sell Stop activates a sale when its condition is met, commonly below the current market. The trigger and the eventual execution price are separate values.

Stop Limit:

  • Buy Stop Limit creates a limit purchase order after activation. The limit constrains the permitted price, but the order may remain unfilled.
  • Sell Stop Limit creates a limit sale order after activation. If available bids are below the limit, it may fail to close the position.

The optional video, Limit and market orders, demonstrates the order-book mechanics. The written explanations above cover execution conditions and price limits.

Reading the order book

The bid is the highest available quoted buying price, and the ask is the lowest available quoted selling price. Their difference is the spread. Last is the price of the most recent completed trade. The displayed order book belongs to the selected instrument and data source.

Displayed limit orders can be filled, changed or canceled. Stop orders usually wait for a separate trigger and need not appear in the visible book. A displayed order does not reveal the owner's identity or the full size of their trading interest.

How orders affect price

Available limit orders provide quantity against which other orders can execute. When that quantity trades or is canceled, the best available prices can change. An order book does not guarantee that a price level will hold.

In my chart analysis, I treat available limit quantity as a possible obstacle to a move, while activated stop-market orders can add orders seeking execution. A price bar cannot identify every order owner or prove that somebody deliberately collected stops. I therefore assess the subsequent reaction: did price continue beyond the level or return to the range?

Leverage affects the funds required to support a position. The next chapter separates those funds from the money the position can lose.

What is leverage?

Before using leverage, separate three things: the funds held to support a position, the position's size, and the conditions under which it can be forcibly closed.

Leverage allows the value of a position to exceed the funds committed to support it. For example, a $10,000 position supported by $1,000 of capital gains or loses about $100 for a 1% price move, before costs, for a product whose result changes directly with price. Selecting a leverage setting does not mean that this position has already been opened.

The effect on your capital grows when you actually increase the position. If its value is 100 times your capital, an adverse move of about 1% can correspond to that capital before costs. Forced closure can occur sooner. The leverage offered by the platform is not an instruction to use the maximum available size.

A margin call warns of insufficient funds or requires additional funds to support the position. Forced closure may be described as a stop-out or liquidation, depending on the product. The provider's rules determine the thresholds and procedure; not every product gives the same warning before closing a position.

For initial practice, I suggest little or no leverage where the instrument permits it. The funds available to support a position still do not determine an acceptable position size. First choose the money you are prepared to lose, identify the stop, then calculate the quantity. Practice that sequence on a demo account.

Choose a risk percentage with possible losing streaks, other open positions and account size in mind. The 1% used in a worked example is an input to that example. It is neither a universally safe limit nor a rule followed by every professional trader.

What is technical analysis?

Technical analysis studies price movements and recurring chart structures. In this course, it provides consistent ways to identify a trend, a level and the condition that would invalidate a trade. A familiar shape does not make its next outcome certain.

Start by distinguishing a trend from a trading range.

Trends and trading ranges

The course's basic chart analysis separates directional movement, called a trend, from sideways movement within a range. That distinction determines which trade conditions to check.

In a trading range, price moves between selected boundaries. It can briefly cross one of them, so judge whether the range still holds from the subsequent reaction, rather than from a touch alone.

The lower boundary is commonly described as support and the upper boundary as resistance. The first diagram shows a range with these boundaries marked.

A trend is directional movement identified through successive highs and lows. My basic trading plan follows that direction. Impulses and pullbacks can vary in length, and the presence of a trend does not guarantee a profitable entry.

The two trend directions are:

  • A downtrend has lower highs and lower lows on the selected timeframe. A temporary rebound does not necessarily break that sequence.
  • An uptrend has higher highs and higher lows on the selected timeframe.

I first identify the structure on the working timeframe. With falling highs and lows, I consider sales from resistance. With rising highs and lows, I consider purchases from support. Priority change levels help identify when the existing plan needs to be reconsidered. This is the course's approach, rather than a compulsory method for every trader.

Priority change levels

A priority change level, or PCL, is the price zone from which an impulse begins that takes price beyond a previous high or low. The zone changes as new movements create new extremes.

In my approach, I mark the origin of that move, then examine whether the zone holds or breaks as the structure develops. The level does not identify the trader responsible for the movement.

The first diagram shows a PCL in a downtrend. Crossing it draws attention to a possible change of direction. To assess a new uptrend, check whether the zone holds and whether higher highs and lows follow. One crossing is insufficient.

For a zone that repeatedly holds in this method, I look for several returns, usually three or more touches. They show repeated price reactions. They do not prove that a particular participant has a hidden position or that the next touch will hold.

The EUR/USD illustration marks zones from which price moved to new lows or highs.

During the descending sequence, each new low led us to mark the relevant resistance zone. Later, a range formed, and an upward structure developed after price left it. The working plan changes when the observed structure changes; we do not treat an anticipated reversal as an established fact.

While the downward structure remains, I consider sales from resistance. After a confirmed change in structure, I check the conditions for purchases from support instead of continuing to apply the old plan automatically.

Reversal patterns

A reversal pattern is a sequence worth checking for a change in the existing structure. It is not a promise that one shape will produce a particular price move.

The diagrams show former resistance becoming a possible support zone after a breakout. Check whether the new zone holds and the movement continues.

In the first illustration, the descending sequence stops making new lows. The linked false-breakout lesson explains a return after a move below a low. Here, the next checks are whether price crosses the marked PCL, holds the zone, and forms higher lows.

A trading range can develop before a reversal, but it is not required. In my analysis, a PCL break prompts a review of the plan; the return to the zone and the subsequent opposite structure determine whether the plan changes.

In the next diagram, price breaks former resistance, holds the new support and forms higher lows. This sequence changes the working scenario. A brief move beyond the level without holding it does not establish a new trend. Apply the same structural checks to the other illustrated reversal shapes.

The method changes the scenario after an observable change in structure. Weakness in the old movement is a reason to check, not evidence that the opposite trend has already formed.

Now compare the movement’s strength before treating a possible turn as established.

Trend strength

Compare the size and speed of an impulse with its pullback on the same timeframe. Smaller impulses combined with larger pullbacks are a reason to examine a weakening structure. They are not an order to enter the opposite direction.

A weaker impulse can still continue the trend if the pullback has not broken its structure. A pause alone does not establish that the opposite side has taken control.

The course's decision requires a structural change. After price crosses the PCL, check whether it holds beyond the level and how it reacts on a return.

Volume analysis

Volume analysis compares the quantity traded with the movement in price. For an exchange-traded futures contract, volume counts contracts traded, rather than the number of separate trades. Check the units supplied by your chart's data source. Tick volume counts quote updates instead of contracts.

In my approach, volume helps assess trading activity alongside levels and the subsequent price reaction. A volume bar does not identify the traders or reveal their next decisions.

Before comparing bars, check the following:

  • Higher volume means more units traded than in the comparison period. Keep the instrument, data source and session comparable; changing a futures contract can also affect the comparison.
  • Lower volume means less activity in those same units. It does not prove that large participants have left the market or predict a sudden move.

The Volume indicator in the illustration lets us compare activity across bars. A taller bar does not necessarily mean more separate trades. In the Wyckoff method, we check the price reaction that follows before interpreting possible accumulation or distribution. The optional video, Wyckoff method, illustrates this comparison.

Reading price and volume at a level

The following Euro futures example illustrates how I interpret a reaction at a level. We can observe price and volume, but the chart does not identify the owners of individual orders.

The diagram shows a move beyond a marked level followed by a return, commonly called a false breakout. We use the order mechanics explained earlier to consider what might have contributed to the move.

A sale requires a buyer, and a purchase requires a seller. When an incoming order is large relative to the quantity available on the other side, it may execute at several prices. This helps explain why we examine unusually active areas. It does not mean all large traders share one valuation or plan.

A false breakout can accompany triggered stops, news or changes in available orders. Its shape alone does not prove that someone deliberately moved price to trigger other traders' stops.

3. A wide falling bar then moves below nearby support. Check whether the lower-high and lower-low structure continues after a rebound, rather than entering solely because one bar fell.

The Wyckoff method

The Wyckoff method examines price and volume across trends and trading ranges. In this course, I use it to compare observed effort with price movement, then check changes in structure. The optional video, Volume-analysis strategy, illustrates how these observations are combined.

First, learn to compare effort with result. Then apply that comparison to the entry conditions.

Effort and result in the Wyckoff method

Effort and result compares trading activity with the progress made by price. Volume provides the activity measure. The bar's range, closing price and subsequent movement show the result.

Read these together on the same timeframe. A large volume bar matters differently when price continues to advance than when it makes little progress and returns to the previous range.

This comparison can help examine:

  • whether successive impulses still advance the trend;
  • how far a wave moves relative to its activity;
  • where an individual bar closes and what happens after it.

Heavy activity with little progress may suggest resistance to the move. Heavy activity with an expanding move shows more progress. Neither observation reveals the participants' identities or the size of their remaining positions.

The gold futures illustration shows the sequence used in this course. It is a worked analysis, not evidence that the next similar pattern must be profitable.

Zone 1: price rises on high volume, then stops advancing and forms a range. Compare the initial activity with that later result. It gives us a reason to consider selling pressure. Total volume cannot show that the number of units sold exceeded the number bought: each executed trade has both sides.

In this method, a stalled rise may suggest supply, while a stalled decline may suggest demand. The surrounding structure and subsequent reaction matter; high volume alone does not establish professional buying or selling.

Zone 2: price fails to hold above the first bar's area and returns to the range. This is the false breakout. Resistance then forms and price begins making lower lows. We can observe the failed move without claiming to know any particular trader's intentions.

Zone 3: a downward impulse develops with lower highs and lower lows. In my plan, this leads me to check for a sale on a pullback, subject to the level and risk conditions. A decline may leave too little room to a suitable target for an acceptable trade.

Together, the stalled rise, failed breakout and new downward structure support a possible distribution interpretation. The sequence supplies evidence for that interpretation; it does not turn it into certainty.

Use effort and result to form a hypothesis. Check the entry conditions separately.

How to find an entry point

Once we have marked a level and examined volume and price reaction, we can define an entry condition.

In this course's system, I wait for a pullback after an impulse, then check for renewed movement in the direction of the new structure.

A pullback is a temporary move against that direction. It can have several causes, including traders closing positions. Stops triggered during a breakout may also generate orders. The chart alone cannot show that one group's profit-taking caused the entire return.

The first diagram illustrates a return to the selected area. The second illustrates a different sequence: price continues without that return. If the pullback does not occur, the planned entry condition has not occurred either. Do not force the live chart to match the illustration you prefer.

Return to wave 3 in the gold example.

The wide falling bar and high volume show an expanding downward move. They do not tell us how much activity came from stops or who sold. The next step is to look for a pullback toward the broken area.

I wait for price to return and examine its reaction. If it does not return, there may be no entry under this plan. I do not chase the move simply because the earlier breakout looked strong.

Holding below the broken area and beginning a new downward impulse support the selling scenario. A return above it calls for a review. These are observable conditions, rather than promises about the next price.

In the illustrated example, price returns toward the consolidation area on much lower volume and resumes its decline. The entry follows the formation of a new priority change level within the downward structure.

Trading psychology

Once you have chosen the stop and calculated the position size, the next task is to follow that plan. Increasing the quantity out of frustration or removing the stop can expose the account to a larger loss than you calculated. The linked trading psychology article explains how emotions can lead to these decisions.

We will calculate position size, then examine the decisions that can disrupt the calculation. Later chapters explain the supporting role of indicators and news in this course's system.

Risk management sets the amount you are prepared to lose before entry. Experience does not make an oversized position safe. The quantity must fit the stop distance, available capital and possible sequence of losing trades.

Choose the risk before placing the trade, rather than increasing it because this entry feels particularly convincing.

Your account funding and planned risk should meet two conditions:

  • You can follow the plan without feeling compelled to recover a loss immediately.
  • Losing the money would not put essential living expenses or other financial commitments at risk.

In the diagram, the planned stop sits beyond the high or low whose breach would invalidate the trade's structure. Its distance determines position size. Do not move it closer merely to justify a larger position. A stop is an execution instruction; costs and available execution prices can change the eventual loss.

Next, calculate the quantity.

How to calculate position size

Position size is the quantity you buy or sell. In this hypothetical example, calculate how many contracts fit the chosen money risk.

The inputs are:

  • Account capital: $35,000.
  • Planned risk: 1% of that capital.
  • Distance from entry to stop: 150 points.
  • Value of a one-point move: $10 per contract.

First convert the percentage to money: $35,000 × 0.01 = $350. The planned price loss per contract is 150 × $10 = $1,500, before costs.

Position size in contracts = money risk ÷ planned loss per contract.

The theoretical quantity is $350 ÷ $1,500 ≈ 0.233 contracts. The answer is in contracts because the point value was specified for one contract.

Permitted quantities

Now check which quantities the exchange permits.

If the contract trades only in whole units, rounding down gives zero contracts. One contract would risk $1,500 at this stop, exceeding the $350 limit. Under these assumptions, skip the trade.

A smaller contract may offer a suitable quantity, but calculate again using its own point value and minimum size. Do not shorten the stop arbitrarily to make the arithmetic fit. Costs and slippage, the difference between the expected and actual execution price, can still increase the loss.

Before calculating, check the instrument's point value, result currency and permitted quantity increments. Practice both the calculation and order entry on a demo account. Do not transfer one product's specifications to another.

With the quantity calculated, examine the decisions that can undo this plan.

Fear and greed in trading

In my approach, fear and greed help explain decisions such as chasing a fast move or refusing to close a losing trade. This is a psychological interpretation, not a claim that two emotions explain every price. The practical task is to preserve the risk chosen before entry.

Treat a demo trade as practice in following an observable condition. Making money is a trading objective, but discipline alone cannot make unprofitable rules profitable.

Emotions can change a decision. Errors in how we assess information can also affect us when we feel calm.

Cognitive biases in trading

Cognitive biases are recurring errors in judging information. The disposition effect describes closing a winning position too quickly while holding a losing one too long. Compare actual exits with your written rules: did you cancel a protective order or take profit before the planned condition?

Overconfidence can encourage extra trades and underestimation of losses. Check whether entries without a valid condition increased and how their costs affected the account. An illusion of control arises when a familiar chart pattern or convenient tool makes the next outcome feel more predictable than it is.

Confirmation bias means giving more attention to evidence supporting your position than to evidence against it. Write down the condition that would invalidate the trade before entering. Anchoring means allowing a reference such as the entry price to dominate the decision despite new information. Herd behavior means treating other people's agreement as evidence: check your own entry condition instead of counting supporters.

How to reduce the effect of biases

Written rules limit decisions made on impulse. Plan the stop before entry and place the protective order as the platform permits. The order can still be canceled and cannot guarantee an exact execution price, so record any change that increases the position's risk.

In your journal, record the result, the reason for entry and your state of mind when deciding. Review these together to identify repeated departures from the plan. Automation can reduce discretionary button presses, but it cannot correct bad rules or remove the possibility of technical failures.

In a 2026 journal article coauthored with Professor Inna Sytnyk, Psychology of investment decisions: cognitive biases of retail traders in financial markets, we discuss cognitive biases in trading decisions. The linked publication provides the discussion and its context; it does not establish that written rules remove every bias.

Using trading indicators

An indicator displays a calculation based on selected data. To use it, understand the calculation, period and signal condition, rather than relying on a line's color or an arrow.

After a losing trade, check the rules followed and the market conditions. Changing the indicator after every loss prevents a meaningful comparison of one system across similar trades.

I spent several years searching for an indicator to predict the market. In this course, I explain why indicators now play a supporting role in my approach: I first check structure, volume and price reaction.

Averaging prices can delay a signal relative to a price change, but not all indicators use the same calculation. Compare their inputs and periods. The name of an indicator is not a prediction guarantee.

Write the signal as an observable condition: for example, which line crosses another and whether the bar has closed. That lets you compare the same event instead of changing its interpretation after the outcome.

Keep an indicator only if you can explain what it adds to structure, priority change levels and volume. Another line without a purpose makes the entry harder to evaluate.

News and economic data can also affect the trading decision. Next, consider their role as context.

Fundamental analysis in trading

Fundamental analysis studies conditions that affect an asset's value and economic activity. For currencies, these may include interest rates and inflation; for a company, revenue and financial obligations. Distinguish the reported figure from what traders expected beforehand.

Interpreting news requires the data, expectations and surrounding conditions. A beginner need not start with a detailed economic model, but should check the timing of important releases when assessing risk and execution.

I suggest learning chart and volume analysis first so you can apply this course's rules consistently. This is a teaching sequence, not a claim that price already reflects every future announcement. New information can change the market.

A reaction depends on how the figure compares with expectations, as well as other events occurring at the time. A similar announcement can therefore accompany different price movements.

Separate the news from the order decision. An announcement does not specify an entry, quantity or stop. A trade still needs the system's conditions.

A trading system

Worked chart example: Euro futures

On this Euro futures chart, check volume, price reaction and the change in structure around a priority change level. The contract is quoted in dollars per euro; its specifications and execution differ from a retail forex product. Numbers mark the sequence of observations on this 15-minute chart. Test the conditions separately before using another timeframe.

1. A wide falling bar has high volume, then price stops making new lows. In the Wyckoff method, I consider possible accumulation. The visible evidence shows a stalled decline, but does not identify the buyers.

2. A range forms. Another attempt to fall does not break the earlier low, and activity during the decline decreases. This supports the accumulation hypothesis, which still needs the subsequent price reaction.

3. Price breaks the marked resistance. This is an observable change in structure. The breakout alone does not replace the pullback and continuation checks.

4. On the pullback, price holds above former resistance and forms a higher low with a bullish pin bar. The linked candlestick lesson explains that bar shape. The decision rests on the sequence, rather than one candle. I plan the stop beyond the low whose breach would invalidate the buying scenario, then check step 5.

5. Price begins another upward move with higher volume. Together, steps 1 through 5 give us a buying setup under this course's rules, provided the target leaves enough room and the position size fits the risk. They do not guarantee further gains.

With the new upward structure established, this plan looks for trend-following purchases from support, as in the earlier diagrams.

Worked chart example: Japanese yen futures

This chart shows Japanese yen futures. Check the symbol and quotation units: the CME contract expresses the yen's value in dollars, whereas USD/JPY expresses the dollar's value in yen. Do not transfer the direction of one quotation to the other. Read the contract actually shown and follow areas 1 through 5.

1. A high-volume decline stops making new lows. I consider a possible selling climax, a period of intense selling followed by a stall. Further events are needed before interpreting accumulation.

2. Price forms a range and the next attempt to decline weakens. This checks the response to the initial event; lower volume by itself does not promise a rise.

3. Price moves above marked resistance. I then wait for the pullback from point 3 to point 4 and check the level. Without that pullback, this entry may not occur.

4. The pullback holds above the broken area and leaves a higher low. I plan the stop beyond it and check for continuation at point 5 instead of deciding from one low.

5. Price resumes its rise with increased activity. After this response to the pullback, check the distance to the target, position size and costs before entering.

The same sequence is shown on a different contract. If a required step is missing, this model has not produced its planned entry. It provides a condition we can check, rather than a guaranteed outcome.

We begin with trades in the direction of the structure. I use a planned risk-to-reward ratio of at least 1:3 as a selection condition when the target leaves enough room: the potential gain is at least three times the planned loss. That ratio alone does not establish positive expectancy, the average result expected across repeated trades. Win frequency, actual exits and costs also matter. Range trading and Price Action, the interpretation of price behavior, belong to later practice after the basic sequence.

Worked chart example: distribution in Euro futures

The earlier Euro example examined possible accumulation. Here we examine distribution and a change from an upward to a downward structure. Numbers 1 through 5 mark observations for this particular contract and chart interval, rather than all EUR/USD trading.

1. An advance on high volume stalls. In the Wyckoff method, I consider a possible buying climax, intense buying followed by a stalled rise, and a distribution hypothesis. Total volume does not identify the sellers.

2. A range forms and another attempt to rise fails to make a new high. The weaker continuation supports the hypothesis but is not a sell entry by itself.

3. Price moves below support. I wait for the pullback from point 3 to point 4 to check whether former support holds as resistance.

4. The pullback leaves a lower high below the broken area. I plan the stop beyond that high, where the selling scenario would be invalidated, then check for a renewed decline.

5. A new downward move has increased volume. It shows continuation after the pullback test. The chart cannot tell us exactly how many sell stops were triggered. Entry still requires an acceptable position size and enough room to the target.

Both buying and selling examples follow the same checks: heavy activity and a stall, a range, a breakout, a return to test the area, then continuation. The shape and duration can differ. We wait for the sequence rather than predicting it in advance.

After entry, apply the exit and position-management rules chosen before the trade.

How to manage and exit a trade

An entry is only part of the plan. Before trading, choose the protective exit, target and conditions for managing the position.

Record these in advance so you can compare the actual exit with the plan, instead of judging it against the best price visible afterward.

Uncertainty means the future price is unknown. New information can change the structure and available execution prices. You can know your exit instruction beforehand without knowing its exact execution result.

A trading “holy grail” would predict the market correctly every time. Searching for it distracts from testing specific rules. Indicators do not remove the need to limit risk. Neither does martingale, increasing the amount risked on the next trade after a loss, nor scalping, taking frequent short-term trades. The linked lessons explain these approaches; here, we define the entry and its invalidation, then assess repeated decisions.

The Wyckoff method helps me compare a level, volume and the subsequent reaction. Commitments of Traders reports can provide additional context: they summarize open positions by participant category at a reporting date and are published afterward. They do not reveal each fund's current plan or prove that hedgers are always right.

Smart Money is an interpretation of large participants’ behavior, not a promise about the next price. I use a significant opposite price zone as a target reference. A statistical pattern means a tendency observed across a defined sample, rather than a certain outcome for the next trade. Whether trades reach the zone often enough requires a review of actual results. Before entering, compare the target distance with the stop distance and intervening price zones.

Exit discipline means following the written conditions. If a new structure forms, apply the chosen management rule. Do not change it simply because you hope to avoid a loss.

After the trade, compare the action with the plan. A planned loss and a rule violation require different responses.

How I manage a position and take profit

The entry follows the move from point 4 to point 5 in the preceding diagrams. Before entering, I identify the stop, target and condition that would invalidate the trade. They are decisions made in advance, rather than improvised as price moves.

For a purchase, I plan the stop beyond the higher low at point 4; for a sale, beyond the lower high. A move beyond that extreme invalidates the scenario. The stop level cannot guarantee an execution price, so record the actual loss separately.

I mark the nearest significant opposite price zone before entry. Projecting the length of the earlier impulse is only a reference, which I compare with actual chart levels. Price does not have to repeat that length. If the nearest obstacle leaves too little room for the chosen reward relative to risk, I skip the trade.

A limit order at the target can specify an acceptable exit price, but it may fill partially or remain unfilled. The order choice belongs to the management plan; it is not evidence that a large participant holds a position at that level.

After a confirmed structural change in the trade's favor, I consider moving the stop toward the entry price under my management rule. That does not guarantee a zero money result: commission, spread and slippage may leave a loss. Do not move the stop farther against the position simply to give a losing trade more room.

After exiting, record whether the plan was followed, what changed in the structure and which costs arose. Consistent rules allow comparison; repetition alone does not produce a profitable system.

The journal records actual exits and costs. Next, use a hypothetical model to examine how win frequency and outcome size affect the average result.

Expectancy: the average result of a trading system

This hypothetical model combines the planned loss, potential gain and proportion of winning trades. Both examples assume identical position size and exact execution at the stop and target. Costs are excluded initially. The win frequencies are chosen for the calculation, not measured performance. Three inputs matter:

  • Stop:
    choose the protective exit before entry. Its actual fill may differ from the selected level. Do not cancel protection in the hope of a recovery.
  • Risk-to-reward ratio:
    compare the planned loss with the potential gain. Here they are 5 and 15 points, giving 1:3. This is a model assumption and my selection condition, not proof of profitability.
  • Win rate:
    the proportion of trades that close with a gain. Together with win size, loss size and costs, it determines the average result. One successful chart example cannot establish it.

Hypothetical examples

First example, with a 1:3 ratio:

  • Planned loss per losing trade: 5 points.
  • Planned gain per winning trade: 15 points.
  • Ten trades: four losses and six wins, a 60% win rate.
  • Losses: 4 × (−5) = −20 points.
  • Gains: 6 × 15 = 90 points.
  • Total: 70 points before costs, or 7 points per trade on average.

Keep the same ratio but change the win rate to 40%:

  • Ten trades: six losses and four wins.
  • Losses: 6 × (−5) = −30 points.
  • Gains: 4 × 15 = 60 points.
  • Total: 30 points before costs, or 3 points per trade on average, under the same position-size and execution assumptions.

In this model, one 15-point gain offsets three 5-point losses. Before costs, a positive average requires a win rate above 25%. Costs raise that threshold. An actual sequence can differ from either hypothetical example.

Losing sequences

Losing trades can occur consecutively even when the average result is positive. Smaller risk reduces the damage from a losing sequence; it cannot make bad rules profitable. Check actual gains, losses and costs in the journal, and limit risk before a sequence prevents further trading. Common beginner trading mistakes

During practice, check for these departures from the plan. Each changes risk or makes the rules harder to evaluate.

  • Trading without a stop leaves the position without the planned protective exit. Price need not return to entry, and a large loss can outweigh several previous gains. The linked position-management lesson explains protective exits.
  • Adding to a losing position without a plan increases the loss if price continues against it. In this course's system, we do not average down simply in the hope of recovery: risk is chosen before entry.
  • Overtrading means taking extra trades without the required conditions. Write down the reason before sending an order. If the condition is absent, do not open a new position.
  • I do not sell solely because price has risen for a long time. A change of direction requires evidence of a new structure; elapsed time alone is not an entry condition.
  • Trying to win back a loss can lead to larger positions and entries without a valid condition. After a rule violation, pause new entries and review the risk calculation and recorded reason for trading.
  • Moving to real money without reviewing practice leaves the rules untested. One hundred demo trades are my initial assignment, not proof of profitability. Use consistent rules, a complete journal and a review of costs.

Knowing a rule is different from following it when money or frustration is involved. That is why this course includes psychology and risk management alongside chart analysis.

The provider's terms affect the products, costs and execution available to you. Check them separately from the chart setup.

How to choose a broker

A broker or other trading provider gives access to transactions under a contract. Execution on an exchange can differ from an over-the-counter product, where the transaction follows the provider's terms rather than a central exchange order book. Identify the legal entity, product and agreement first. Check the following before funding:

  • Regulation: find the exact entity in the regulator's register and match its authorized services, contact details and the jurisdiction of your agreement. A license does not cover market losses or guarantee recovery of every deposit. A clone company may misuse a real firm's license number.
  • Instruments: confirm that the required shares, futures, currency or crypto product is available and read its specifications.
  • Order execution: check stop triggers, partial fills and price-gap rules. A demo tests the interface; it cannot establish the future live order queue or slippage.
  • Costs: compare entry, exit and financing costs for the same position. Frequent trading repeats transaction costs.
  • Platform: use the demo to check order entry, position display and data access. A convenient interface does not establish live execution quality.
  • Support: check the available contact channels and whether answers explain the product's terms before opening a funded account.

Read the agreement and charges, match the company to the register, and test the interface. A particular number of weeks on demo cannot verify protection of funds or future execution.

Then select one instrument for initial practice.

What should a beginner trade?

Start with one instrument and understand its quotation, permitted quantity and costs. The main categories differ in important ways.

  • Futures are exchange-traded contracts with defined specifications and an expiry. Some settle through delivery, others in cash. Their exchange volume belongs to that particular contract. Exchange rules do not remove every broker-related risk. The course uses futures examples to compare exchange-reported volume.
  • Forex is the over-the-counter currency market. A retail product may be a CFD or another agreement with a provider, rather than direct access to interbank trading. There is no single volume total for the entire forex market; check the chart's source and units.
  • Shares represent ownership in a company. Trading access, costs and short-selling terms depend on the venue and provider. Assess the capital needed for a particular instrument rather than an entire market category.
  • Cryptocurrencies can trade around the clock. Volatility, the size of price fluctuations, varies across coins and periods. Check whether you are buying a coin or trading a contract, which venue supplies volume and how funds are held. Limited quantities available at quoted prices can affect execution.

Choose one accessible instrument, check its specifications, demo terms and liquidity. You can study structure and volume on different markets, but test the rules on the actual data used. One hundred practice trades are an initial sample, not automatic permission to risk real money.

Begin practice on the selected instrument.

How to start trading on a demo account

Connect a virtual account and select one instrument. Before each trade, mark the structure, entry condition, stop, target and permitted quantity. After exiting, record the result and whether you followed the rules. Review mistakes before the next attempt rather than hiding them from the journal.

Begin with at least 100 demo trades under consistent rules. Review departures from the plan as well as the total result. Changing the market, timeframe or execution conditions requires another assessment. A positive demo result does not promise future profits.

Record the planned and actual entry and exit prices, together with costs, for each demo trade. Comparing them makes differences in execution visible instead of judging the plan from the final profit or loss alone.

Keeping a trading journal

A trading journal records every trade, including accidental entries and rule violations. Mark which trades followed the system so those can be evaluated separately. Do not delete inconvenient records: doing so hides actual risk and changes the reported account result.

Estimate the system's average result from trades following the chosen rules. Review actual entries, exits, costs and adherence after the practice series. Its average does not guarantee the same future result. Moving to a funded account requires a separate risk decision.

What to record

For each trade, record:

  • Date and time: when the position opened.
  • Instrument: the exact symbol and contract, where relevant.
  • Direction: long or short.
  • Entry: the actual opening price.
  • Stop: the protective order's planned level.
  • Target: the planned profit-taking level.
  • Result: actual fills and quantity, plus gain or loss before and after costs.
  • Reason for entry: the observed condition, such as a priority change level, selling climax or level breakout.

If you cannot explain the entry, label it as accidental or a rule violation. Record what happened and which rule needs to be restored.

Reviewing the record

Analyze rule-following trades separately when assessing the system. Calculate the overall account result from every trade, so an accidental loss does not disappear.

Review comparable conditions, costs, violations and losing sequences regularly. A small winning group does not establish an advantage. If you change the rules, mark the new version separately.

Frequently asked questions

Can I learn trading for free?

Yes. This course's learning materials are available free for independent study. They explain the trading platform, analysis rules, risk and demo practice in sequence. Completing them does not guarantee professional qualification or profits.

How long does it take to learn trading?

It depends on your starting knowledge and practice time. Work through the chapters, then complete and review the practice series. One hundred trades are the initial assignment; there is no fixed deadline that makes a learner ready for real money.

What does a 1:3 risk-to-reward ratio mean?

The planned gain to the target is three times the planned loss to the stop. In this course's hypothetical example, that means 15 and 5 points. The overall result also depends on win frequency, costs and actual execution.

What is Smart Money in trading?

Smart Money is an approach to interpreting large participants' activity through price, volume and the quantity available to trade. The interpretation does not establish who caused a particular bar or whether it was intentional manipulation. The existing linked lesson explains the approach.

Why do traders lose money?

Losses can result from oversized positions, canceled stops, unplanned additions to losing trades and entries without tested conditions. A written plan helps control these decisions, but following it does not ensure that the rules will make money.

How much money do I need to start trading?

Begin practicing on a demo account using virtual funds. A real position requires enough funds for the product's minimum quantity, the amount required to support the position, and chosen money risk. The same risk percentage is not appropriate for every account and system.

What is a priority change level (PCL)?

A PCL is an area from which a directional price move begins that goes beyond a selected previous high or low. In this course's system, it is a reference for structure. Check the subsequent reaction to assess whether the area holds or breaks.

How can I identify a false breakout?

A false breakout occurs when price moves beyond a marked level and returns. High volume or a candle pattern may accompany it, but neither is required by the definition. A trade under this course's rules requires separate checks of structure and risk.
