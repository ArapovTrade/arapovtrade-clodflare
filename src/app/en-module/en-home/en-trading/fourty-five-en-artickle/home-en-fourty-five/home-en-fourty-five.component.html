Bars and Japanese candlesticks: what is the difference?

A standard bar and a standard Japanese candlestick show the open, close, high and low for one period. Their appearance differs. Synthetic chart types may calculate other values, so choose standard bars or candles for the examples.

On a bar, the left tick marks the open, the right tick the close, and the vertical line the high and low. A candle’s body connects the open and close; its wicks mark the extreme prices. Colors depend on the platform settings.

We use bars in the course examples.

A bar shows price movement over a selected period. Four values are needed to read it:

  • C (Close): closing price.
  • H (High): highest price during the period.
  • L (Low): lowest price during the period.

A bullish bar closes above its open. Green is a display setting. The bar shows a price rise during the period, but does not prove that there were more buyers: every executed trade has both sides.

Trading basics: spread, lot, long and short

Spread and commission

The platform is open and the demo account is ready. We will cover three basics: the cost of entering the market (spread), the units used for quantity (lot), and the direction you can trade (long or short).

Immediately after entry, a position may show a loss because of spread and commission. Check the execution prices and how the platform calculates the result.

The spread is the difference between the best buying and selling quotes. If you buy and immediately sell, this difference affects the result. It is not necessarily charged as a separate commission.

You can buy the euro at 1.0850 and sell it at 1.0848. The difference is 0.0002, or 2 pips using the standard 0.0001 pip for this pair. Buying and immediately selling at these prices includes that difference in the loss. The monetary result depends on position size; it is not always a separate deduction from the account at entry.

Commission is a separate charge for transactions. It may be fixed or depend on quantity; holding a position may incur additional costs. Before trading, find out what is included in the spread and which fees are charged separately.

What is a lot in trading?

A lot is a defined unit of quantity. The platform may accept lots, contracts, shares or coins: always check the field label and product specifications.

Under a common Forex specification, a standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency. Check the availability of smaller quantities and the quantity increment with your chosen company:

  • 1 lot = 100,000 units.
  • 0.1 lot = 10,000 units (mini lot).
  • 0.01 lot = 1,000 units (micro lot).

Exchange-traded futures trade in whole contracts. Point value is set by the specifications of the particular contract. Do not automatically transfer fractional currency-lot sizes to futures: you cannot open 0.233 of an exchange-traded contract.

Even on a demo account, choose quantity based on predetermined risk. The instrument’s minimum available size may still be too large for your account; then skip the trade or consider another available instrument.

Long and short positions

A short position can benefit from a price decline. How you open it depends on the instrument: borrowing shares and selling a futures contract work differently.

A long position is intended to benefit from rising prices. Buying at one price and selling at a higher one produces a positive difference before costs. A price decline produces a loss.

A short position is intended to benefit from falling prices. To short borrowed shares, you borrow them, sell them and later buy them back to return them. In futures and CFDs (contracts for difference), the position is created under the contract’s terms; borrowing the asset itself is not a required step.

In a hypothetical EUR/USD short, entry is at 1.0850 and exit at 1.0800. The favorable movement is 50 standard pips before costs. Monetary profit depends on quantity and product specifications; these prices do not establish that euros were borrowed.

Long and short describe direction. An order specifies the instruction to open or close a position.

Types of exchange orders

An order is an instruction to buy or sell a specified quantity of an instrument. Begin by distinguishing a market order, a limit price and a stop’s activation condition. Available types depend on the product and venue.

Market order

A market order is intended to execute against available opposing quotes. The displayed quote does not guarantee the price of the whole trade; partial execution, delays or venue-specific restrictions are possible.

  • Buy: purchase against available opposing quotes.
  • Sell: sale against available opposing quotes.

A market order prioritizes execution over a fixed price. Slippage is the difference between the expected price and actual execution. If opposing orders are insufficient, the price may differ substantially from expectations.

Pending order

On the platform, a pending order waits for its specified condition. A limit order restricts execution price and may fill immediately if opposing quotes already qualify. A stop order first waits for an activation condition. Reaching a level alone does not guarantee a full fill.

Limit:

  • Buy Limit sets the maximum purchase price. It is often placed below the market to await a decline, but may execute immediately if the available selling quote is no higher than the limit.
  • Sell Limit sets the minimum selling price. You can leave it above the market to await a rise; if an opposing quote is already at least as favorable as the limit, it may execute immediately.

Stop:

  • Buy Stop sets a buying activation condition above a selected price. With market execution, the final price may differ from the activation level. A breakout does not place an order automatically; a trader or program must submit it.
  • Sell Stop sets a selling activation condition below a selected price. Final execution depends on opposing orders. The activation condition and execution price are different values.

Stop Limit:

  • Buy Stop Limit creates a buy limit order once the activation condition is met. The limit restricts the price, but during fast movement the order may remain unfilled.
  • Sell Stop Limit creates a sell limit order after activation. If available prices are below the limit, the order may remain unfilled. Used to exit a position, it does not guarantee that the position will close.

The order book: how pending orders appear

Bid is the best available buy-order price; Ask is the best sell-order price. Their difference is the spread. Last is the price of the most recent trade. The visible order book belongs to the selected instrument and data source.

The book shows available limit orders. They can be filled, changed or canceled. Stops generally activate under a separate condition and need not appear in the book. A visible order does not reliably identify its owner or their entire quantity.

Limit orders provide opposing quantity for execution. While that quantity remains available, trades occur at the corresponding prices; after orders fill or are canceled, the best quotes may shift. The book does not guarantee that a level will hold.

Leverage affects the collateral required for a position. Separately, check how the selected quantity changes the monetary loss when price moves.

What is leverage in trading?

Before using leverage, we will examine collateral, position size and forced-close conditions.

Leverage lets you open a position whose notional value exceeds the collateral deposited. With $1,000 in capital and a $10,000 position, a 1% price change corresponds to about $100 in profit or loss before costs. Selecting leverage in the settings does not mean that such a position has already been opened.

Leverage magnifies changes in profit or loss relative to capital when the actual position size increases. If the position is 100 times your capital, a roughly 1% adverse move equals that capital before costs. Forced closure may occur earlier.

A margin call reports insufficient funds or requests additional collateral for the position. Forced closure is called stop-out or liquidation, depending on the product. The venue’s terms set the threshold and process; advance warning of forced closure is not guaranteed.

For initial practice, I suggest minimal leverage or no leverage if the instrument allows it. Available collateral is not permission to use the largest possible position. First determine your acceptable loss, then the stop and position size; practice the calculation on a demo account.

Technical analysis of the market

Technical analysis studies price movement and recurring chart structures. In this course, it gives us a consistent way to describe trends, levels and trade-invalidation conditions. A repeating shape does not mean the next outcome is known in advance.

Why switch timeframes?

A timeframe sets the period of one bar. Daily and one-minute charts show different detail; their local trends may point in different directions. Note your chosen timeframe before analyzing the chart.

The practical rule is simple:

  • A higher timeframe, such as a day, helps mark the broader structure and zones. It provides context but does not replace the trade condition on your working timeframe.
  • A lower timeframe, such as 15 minutes, shows movement in detail. Check entry and stop against your rules while keeping the higher-timeframe context in mind.

From bars to waves: reading market structure

Price waves consist of sequences of bars. Within them, we mark impulses, pullbacks, local highs and lows to identify structure.

An impulse is a directional stretch of price movement. A pullback is movement in the opposite direction after an impulse. Local highs and lows are the peaks and troughs of the waves you have identified.

A wave is a selected price movement. It can contain bars in both directions, so its boundaries are marked using selected highs and lows. Alternating impulses and pullbacks help describe structure.

Price movement alternates between impulses and pullbacks. Sometimes movement continues without a convenient pullback; in that case, the planned entry may not occur.

Identifying structure helps choose direction; it does not promise the next price.

Market phases: trends and trading ranges

When marking up a chart in this course, we distinguish directional movement (a trend) from a sideways trading range. This determines which trade conditions we check.

A trading range is sideways movement within selected boundaries. Price can temporarily move beyond a boundary; assess whether the range remains intact by the subsequent reaction.

The upper boundary of a range is commonly called resistance; the lower boundary is support.

A trend is directional movement with a sequence of highs and lows. My basic plan selects trades in its direction. Impulses and pullbacks can have different durations; a trend’s presence alone does not guarantee profit.

There are two main types of trend:

  • A bearish trend has lower highs and lower lows on the chosen timeframe. A single rally does not necessarily invalidate that sequence.
  • A bullish trend has higher highs and higher lows on the chosen timeframe.

First, I identify structure on the chosen timeframe. With successively lower highs and lows, I consider selling from resistance; with higher highs and lows, buying from support. Priority change levels help define when the old scenario should be reconsidered. This is the course’s rule, not a method every trader must use.

Priority change levels

In my approach, a Priority Change Level (PCL) is a dynamic chart zone from which an impulse (a buying or selling wave) begins and goes on to make new price extremes.

Passing through a level draws attention to a possible change of scenario. We check a new uptrend by whether the area holds and highs and lows rise, not by one breakout alone.

To mark an area that holds in this method, I look for repeated returns, usually three or more touches. These show repeated reactions, but do not confirm a particular participant’s hidden position or guarantee another bounce.

On the EUR/USD chart, we will mark areas from which movement produced new lows and highs.

Each time price made a new low, we marked a resistance level. This indicated a bearish market tendency, and until price structure changed, further decline remained our scenario. In this example, a clearly bounded range formed before the trend changed; after price left it, a bullish upward structure began to develop. Remember: until the market itself replaces the current structure with a new one, we do not predict that this will happen! A trader’s job is not to imagine future movement, but to respond to what the market actually shows!

In a downtrend, I look for short entries at resistance. After a confirmed structural change, I check buying conditions at support instead of automatically continuing with the old plan.

How to assess trend strength

To assess strength, compare the impulse’s range and speed with the pullback on the same timeframe. Smaller impulses combined with wider pullbacks are a reason to check for weakening structure, not an entry signal on their own.

Even a weakened impulse can continue the trend if the pullback has not broken its structure. A pause alone therefore does not prove that the opposite side has taken control.

A weakening trend does not yet mean a change of direction. After a PCL break, check whether the level holds and an opposite sequence of highs and lows forms.

Market volume analysis

Volume analysis compares the quantity of instrument units traded with price movement. For an exchange-traded future, this is the number of contracts in executed trades, not the number of individual trades. Check the units for other sources: tick volume counts quote updates.

In my analysis, volume helps form a hypothesis about the activity of large market participants alongside levels and price reactions. A volume bar alone does not reveal participants’ identities, intentions or future actions.

Key aspects of the analysis:

  • Higher volume means more instrument units were traded during the period than in the chosen comparison. Check that the contract, venue, session and source have not changed. This value does not identify the participants.
  • Lower volume means less trading activity in the same units over a comparable period. It does not prove that large capital is absent or promise a sharp move afterward.

Working with levels: a liquidity shortage example

In the following example, we observe price, volume and the subsequent reaction near a level. The owners of the orders and their intentions are not directly visible on the chart.

We will examine a Euro futures example using CME Group data to see how the market reverses at a level after a false breakout.

A sale needs an opposing buyer; a purchase needs an opposing seller. An order that is large relative to available opposing quantity can shift execution prices. In my analysis, this is a reason to observe large activity near a level, not to assume that all large participants value the asset alike.

In my analysis, limit-order quantity can obstruct movement, while activating stop orders can add buy or sell orders; their execution depends on the order type. A bar cannot reveal all order owners or prove that stops were deliberately targeted. I therefore decide after the price reaction: did movement continue beyond the level, or did the market return to the range?

A false breakout is a move beyond a marked level followed by a return. It can involve stop activation, news or changes in available orders. The chart alone does not prove that someone deliberately moved price to trigger other people’s stops.

1. A wide up bar appears near the level. We observe movement and volume; buy-stop activation is one possible explanation, not a visible list of orders. If price stops rising despite high activity, we test the hypothesis of opposing sales. A decision requires the subsequent price reaction.

2. The following up bars have smaller ranges, roughly half the first bar’s range in the image. This is reduced price range, not a measurement of sellers’ identities or the exact size of a hidden position.

3. A wide down bar then passes below local support areas. This is consistent with aggressive selling relative to available buy orders, but the image does not identify the participants. Check whether the lower structure persists after a pullback.

In this example, the observed structure changes near a high-activity level. I use it as an initial area for examining liquidity and the subsequent impulse and pullback. Effort versus result in the Wyckoff method

Effort versus result links trading activity with price progress. Volume describes executed trades in instrument units; we assess the result through the range, close and subsequent movement.

Compare volume with price progress: does activity accompany expanding movement, or does price barely move?

This relationship can be used to analyze:

  • Trend strength.
  • The effectiveness of individual price waves.
  • The behavior of individual bars.

With high volume and little price progress, I consider possible resistance to movement. If activity accompanies expanding movement, I note the achieved result. Neither case establishes participant identities or the exact size of hidden positions.

We will compare volume and price movement on the GC gold futures chart. The image shows a learning sequence, not guaranteed profit.

Zone 1: price rises on high volume, then the rise stalls and a range forms. We compare effort with the subsequent result, not just one bar. This is a reason to test an opposing-supply hypothesis; total volume cannot show that the number of sales exceeded purchases.

In the Wyckoff method, a rise stalling on high activity can be interpreted as supply; a decline stalling can be a possible sign of demand. This interpretation needs context and follow-through: one high-volume reading does not prove professional buying or selling.

Zone 2: price fails to hold above the first bar’s area and returns to the range. This failed move is called a false breakout. Resistance then forms, followed by new lows. It does not establish that a particular participant lost interest.

Zone 3: a downward impulse begins, with new lower highs and lows. In my plan, this is a reason to look for a short entry on a pullback after checking the level and risk. Not every decline leaves enough room to the target.

This sequence shows the method in use: a stall, a failed breakout, then new structure. We test the distribution hypothesis through these events, not through claims about unknown participants’ intentions.

Effort versus result helps form a hypothesis. We check the entry condition separately through the impulse, pullback and reaction at the level.

In the course’s system, I wait for a pullback after an impulse and check for continuation in the new structure’s direction.

A pullback can have various causes, including positions being closed. A breakout can activate stop orders. Their subsequent execution depends on their type and opposing quantity. A chart cannot prove that a return was caused solely by one group taking profits.

Trading psychology

It is important to maintain your predetermined risk. Emotionally increasing quantity or canceling protection can lead to a larger loss than planned. Trading psychology explains why such decisions arise.

Risk management sets the acceptable loss before entry. Experience does not make high risk safe: position size must fit the stop, capital and a possible losing streak.

The amount you fund your account with and your risk per trade must always meet two criteria:

  • They must feel comfortable at your current level of experience.
  • They must not threaten your financial security.

How to size a position for your risk

We plan the stop beyond the extreme whose breach would invalidate the chosen trade structure. The stop distance determines position size, not the reverse. A stop is an execution instruction, so the actual loss can differ because of costs and available prices.

Before calculating, check the point value, the currency in which profit and loss are calculated and permitted quantity increment. Use the selected product’s parameters. Practice calculation and order submission on demo without transferring one instrument’s specifications to another.

For the calculation, we use a hypothetical contract: a one-point price change is worth $10 per contract. A tick is the instrument’s minimum price increment; take its size and monetary value from the specifications.

Inputs:

  • Capital: $35,000.
  • Acceptable risk: 1% of capital.
  • Distance to stop: 150 points.
  • Value of one point: $10 per contract.

Choose the risk percentage with possible losing streaks, total account exposure and account size in mind. The 1% in this exercise is an input, not a universally safe limit or a rule followed by all professionals.

Position-size formula:

Position size = (Capital × Risk percentage × 0.01) / (Risk in points × Point value).

Theoretical size = (35,000 × 1 × 0.01) / (150 × 10) = 350 / 1,500 ≈ 0.233 contracts. The unit is contracts because point value is specified for one contract.

Check the permitted quantity increment:

If the exchange permits only whole contracts, rounding down gives 0 contracts. One contract with this stop has planned risk of 150 × 10 = $1,500, exceeding the $350 limit. We do not open this trade.

Consider a smaller contract or another instrument only after checking its own point value and quantity increment. Do not arbitrarily tighten the stop to obtain the desired number. Even a permitted size does not guarantee the exact loss: costs and slippage can increase it.

Cognitive biases in trading

Cognitive biases are persistent errors in evaluating information. The disposition effect appears when a trader quickly closes a winner for the feeling of success but holds a loser to avoid admitting a mistake. Check your journal for protective orders canceled before execution or profitable trades closed before your own rule allowed.

Overconfidence can lead to more frequent trading and underestimating losses. Check whether entries without valid conditions increase and how costs change the result. The illusion of control appears when a convenient tool or familiar chart shape is mistaken for certainty about the future.

Confirmation bias means seeking only arguments that support your position. Write down the invalidation condition before entry as well. Anchoring ties a decision to the entry price instead of new information.

Herd behavior leads people to treat an idea’s popularity as proof: check your own rule, not the number of people agreeing.

How to reduce the influence of cognitive biases

Written rules leave less room for decisions driven by mood. A stop placed before a trade helps execute the plan, but it can be canceled and does not guarantee an exact price. Check your journal for stops moved farther against the position.

Record the financial result, the reason for entry and your state of mind when deciding. Reviewing records reveals recurring violations. Automating execution reduces emotion-driven decisions when placing orders, but does not fix faulty rules or remove the risk of failures.

Indicators in trading

An indicator displays a calculation based on selected data. To use it meaningfully, understand its formula, period and signal conditions, not just the line or arrow color.

If a trade loses, check rule execution and market conditions. Replacing the indicator after every loss makes it difficult to evaluate one system across a comparable series.

I spent several years searching for an indicator to predict the market. In this course, I explain why indicators play a supporting role for me: I check structure, volume and price reaction first.

Searching for a “holy grail,” a method that always predicts the market, distracts from testing specific rules. Indicators, martingale and scalping do not remove the need to limit risk and assess results. In my teaching sequence, we first define what must happen before entering and what would invalidate the setup, then assess a series of trades.

Price averaging can produce a delayed response, but not all indicators work alike. Compare the calculation, period and data source; an indicator’s name tells you nothing about how accurately it can predict price.

Write the signal as an observable condition: for example, which line crossed which, and on which bar. This lets you compare the same events instead of changing the explanation after the result.

Keep an indicator only if you can state what observation it adds to structure, PCL and volume.

Fundamental analysis in trading

Fundamental analysis studies conditions affecting an asset’s value and operations. For currencies, these may include interest rates and inflation; for a company, income and liabilities. Distinguish the data itself from participants’ expectations.

Interpreting news requires data, market expectations and context. A beginner need not start with a detailed macroeconomic model, but should consider major-event timing when assessing execution and risk.

COT reports can provide additional context: they show aggregate open positions by participant category at a snapshot date and are published afterward. They do not reveal every fund’s current plan or establish that hedgers are always right.

I suggest beginners learn charts and volume first so they can apply the course rules consistently. This is a teaching order, not a claim that price already fully reflects all future news. New information can change the market.

The market reacts to both an indicator’s value and its difference from expectations. The same announcement can therefore accompany different price reactions.

Separate the news from the trading decision: an event announcement does not determine your entry point, quantity or stop.

Building a trading system

Real-chart example: Euro futures

On the Euro futures chart, we will check volume, price reaction and structural change through the PCL. This contract is quoted in dollars per euro; its terms and execution differ from a retail Forex pair. The numbers mark the observation sequence. The example uses a 15-minute interval; test the rules separately on another scale.

1. A wide down bar on the Euro chart is accompanied by high volume; afterward, price stops making new lows. In Wyckoff terms, I consider this a possible beginning of accumulation. The observations confirm that the decline stalled, but do not identify the owners of opposing purchases.

2. A range forms. Another attempt to decline does not break the previous low, while activity on the decline decreases. This is an additional condition for my accumulation hypothesis, which must be tested by the subsequent exit from the range.

3. Price breaks the marked resistance. We now have an observable structural change, not just an accumulation hypothesis. The breakout alone does not replace checking the pullback and subsequent movement.

4. Pullback 3–4 returns to the broken resistance and briefly moves below it. Point 4’s low is above the low of range 2; a bullish pin bar forms there. I plan the stop beyond point 4’s low. After the pullback, I check for price returning above the PCL and continuing at point 5.

5. A new upward move appears after the pullback, with increased volume. Sequence 1–5 supports preparing a buy under the course rules if there is enough room to the target and a permissible position size has been calculated. It does not guarantee further growth.

Once the new structure marked on the chart forms, trades under this approach should follow the trend from support levels, as in the schematic examples.

Real-chart example: Japanese yen futures

This example shows Japanese yen futures. Check the symbol and quote unit: CME yen futures express the yen’s value in dollars, whereas USD/JPY expresses the dollar’s value in yen. Do not treat their quote directions as identical. We will read the contract shown and the sequence of areas 1–5.

1. At point 1, a sharp decline on high volume stalls; range 2 then forms. I consider a possible selling climax, but accumulation needs confirmation from later events.

2. Price forms a range; a further attempt to decline weakens. This checks the reaction after the first event, not a promise of growth whenever volume decreases.

3. Price moves above the marked resistance. In my plan, I then wait for a pullback and level test, wave 3–4. Without a pullback, this entry may not develop.

4. Pullback 3–4 returns below the broken resistance. Point 4’s low remains above the low of range 2. I plan a stop beyond low 4, then check for a return above the PCL and renewed growth at point 5.

5. Upward movement resumes with increased activity. Before opening a trade, I then check the distance to the target, position size and costs.

This repeats the preceding sequence on another contract. If a required stage from 1–5 is absent, this model provides no entry. The sequence gives a testable trade condition, not a guaranteed result.

In this course, we begin by following the direction of structure. I treat trading within a range and Price Action, the interpretation of price behavior, as a later stage after the basic sequence.

Real-chart example: distribution in Euro futures

The previous Euro futures example examined accumulation. On another chart segment, we will check distribution and the transition from upward to downward structure. Numbers 1–5 mark the observation sequence. The data belongs to this contract and chart interval, not to all EUR/USD trades.

1. Growth on high volume stalls on the Euro chart. In Wyckoff terms, I consider a possible buying climax and a distribution hypothesis. Total volume does not identify the sellers.

2. A range forms, and another attempt to rise does not break the high. Weaker continuation supports the hypothesis but is not a standalone selling entry.

3. Price moves below support. After the breakout, I wait for pullback 3–4 to check whether former support holds as resistance.

4. Pullback 3–4 returns to former support and briefly moves above it. Point 4’s high is below the peaks of range 2. I plan the protective stop for the short position above point 4’s high; after the pullback, I check for a return below the area and continuation at point 5.

5. A new downward move is accompanied by increased volume. I observe continuation after test 3–4; this chart does not reveal the exact number of activated sell-stop orders. Entry requires acceptable risk and room to the target.

The buying and selling models use the same checking sequence: high activity and a stall, a range, a breakout, a test and continuation. The shape and duration of the moves can differ.

Position management: how do you exit a trade?

Define the stop, target and invalidation condition before entry. Then management can be compared with the plan instead of judged afterward against the best price.

Uncertainty means the future price is unknown. New information can change available prices and structure. The exit instruction is therefore known in advance, but its exact execution result is not.

I use the marked opposing level as a target reference. How often that target is reached needs testing across a series of trades. Before entry, compare the distance to the target, the stop and intervening obstacles.

Exit discipline means following conditions written in advance. Use the marked target and invalidation condition. If new structure appears, apply your management rule; do not change it merely in the hope of avoiding a loss.

After the trade, compare the action taken with the plan. A loss within the rules and a rule violation call for different conclusions.

How I manage positions and take profits

In the examples above, I check a buy after low 4, when growth resumes toward point 5. The entry area is schematic: the exact execution bar is not marked.

For a long position, I place the stop beyond the higher low at point 4; for a short position, beyond the lower high. A return beyond that extreme invalidates the selected scenario. The stop level does not guarantee execution price, so record the actual loss separately.

Before entry, I mark the nearest significant opposing zone as the target. I use impulse projection only as a reference and compare it with actual levels in the image. Price need not repeat the first move’s length; if the nearest obstacle leaves insufficient room for the selected ratio, I skip the trade.

You can place a limit order in the target area in advance to take profit. It restricts price, but may fill only partly or not at all.

After a confirmed structural change in the trade’s favor, I consider moving the stop to the entry price under my management rule. This does not guarantee a monetary break-even: commission, spread and slippage can leave a loss. Do not move a stop farther against the position in the hope of recovery.

After exit, I record whether the plan was executed, what changed in the structure and which costs arose. Repeating consistent rules helps assess the system, but does not itself produce a positive result.

Trading expectancy: a profitable-system model

This learning model connects a protective exit, the win/loss ratio and the proportion of winning trades. In both examples, quantity is identical, stops and targets execute at the specified prices, and costs are excluded for now. The win rates are inputs to the calculation, not measurements from a real system.

  • In the course’s system, we set the protective exit before entry. A stop limits the planned loss, but execution price can differ from the selected level. Do not cancel protection in the hope of a recovery.
  • The risk/reward ratio compares the planned loss to the stop with the profit to the target. We use a 5-point stop and a 15-point target, giving a ratio of 1:3. This is a model assumption and my selection criterion, not sufficient proof of profitability.
  • Win rate is the proportion of the selected series that closed profitably. Together with win size, loss size and costs, it determines the average result. Do not infer it from one successful example.

I use a risk/reward ratio of at least 1:3 as a trade-selection condition when there is room to the chosen target. It does not guarantee positive expectancy: win frequency, actual exits and costs matter.

An example with a 1:3 risk/reward ratio:

  • Stop-loss: 5 points.
  • Take-profit: 15 points.
  • 10 trades: 4 losers and 6 winners (60% win rate).
  • Losses: 4 × (−5) = −20 points.
  • Gains: 6 × (+15) = +90 points.
  • Total result: +70 points over 10 trades.

The same 1:3 ratio, but a win rate of only 40%:

  • 10 trades: 6 losers and 4 winners.
  • Losses: 6 × (−5) = −30 points.
  • Gains: 4 × (+15) = +60 points.
  • This hypothetical series returns +30 points before costs, with identical size for every trade and execution at the specified prices.

In this model, one 15-point winner covers three 5-point losses. For a positive average before costs, the win rate must exceed 25%; costs raise the required rate. Actual trading results can differ from this exercise.

Losing trades can occur consecutively even with a positive average result. Small risk reduces the damage from a streak but does not create positive expectancy for poor rules. Check actual wins, losses and costs in your journal; limit risk in advance so your account can withstand a losing streak. See “Account Drawdown.”

The calculation shows the model’s conditions, not a future result.

Common mistakes beginners make

In practice, check for these violations: each changes planned risk or prevents a fair assessment of the rules.

  • Trading without a stop-loss leaves no predetermined protective exit. Price need not return to entry. One large loss can outweigh the results of preceding trades.
  • Unplanned additions to a losing position. Adding quantity increases the total loss if price continues against the trade. In this system, we do not average down out of hope: acceptable risk is set before entry.
  • Overtrading means opening unnecessary trades without the required conditions. If the system provides no reason, do not open another position. Write down your reason for entry before submitting the order.
  • In the course’s system, I do not sell just because price has risen for a long time. Changing direction requires observations of new structure. The duration of a rise alone is not a selling condition.
  • Trying to win back a loss. Increasing quantity and entering without conditions add pressure to the account. After a violation, stop opening new trades and review the recorded risk and entry rationale.
  • Moving to a live account without reviewing practice. One hundred practice trades are my initial assignment, but the number itself does not prove profitability. Consistent rules, a complete journal and cost assessment are needed.

Emotions can lead to these mistakes. That is why trading psychology and risk management stand alongside technical analysis in this course.

How to choose a broker

A broker or other trading company provides access to selected transactions under a contract. Execution may differ between a centralized exchange and an OTC product. First check the legal entity, product and agreement.

  • Regulation and licensing. Find the exact legal entity in the regulator’s register; check permitted services, the agreement’s jurisdiction and contact details. A license does not guarantee all funds or cover market losses. A clone company may use another company’s license number.
  • Available instruments. Check access to the required stocks, futures, currency or cryptocurrency product and its specifications.
  • Order execution. Learn the rules for stop activation, partial fills and gaps. A demo account checks the interface, not future order queues or live slippage.
  • Commissions and spreads. Compare the costs of entering, exiting and holding the same position. Frequent trades increase recurring costs.
  • The trading platform. On demo, check that orders are understandable, positions are displayed clearly and data is available. Demo convenience does not establish the quality of live execution.
  • Support quality. Check how quickly and competently support responds before opening a live account. It may matter at a critical moment.

Before funding your account, review the agreement and costs, verify the company against the register and test the demo interface. Weeks spent on demo alone do not establish live-execution quality or protection of funds.

What can a beginner trade?

A beginner is better served by focusing on one instrument and understanding its quote, position size and costs. We will examine the main categories.

  • Futures. Exchange-traded contracts with an expiry and defined specifications. Some provide for delivery; others settle in cash. Exchange volume belongs to a particular contract; venue rules do not eliminate every broker risk. The course uses futures to read comparable exchange volume.
  • Forex. An OTC currency market; a retail product may be a CFD or another contract with a company, not a direct interbank trade. There is no unified volume for all Forex. Check the source and units when reading the indicator.
  • Stocks represent ownership in a company. Available volume data, costs and short-selling conditions depend on the venue and company. Assess required capital for the actual instrument, not the market’s general name.
  • Cryptocurrency may trade around the clock. Volatility, the size of price fluctuations, varies across coins and periods. Check whether you are buying a coin or a contract, what volume data the venue provides and how funds are held. On illiquid instruments, execution may differ from the displayed price.

Start with one available instrument and check its specifications, demo conditions and liquidity. You can study structure and volume in different markets, but test rules on specific data. One hundred practice trades provide an initial sample, not automatic permission to use real money.

The trading journal

Record every trade in the journal, including accidental entries and rule violations. To assess a particular system, flag the trades that followed it. Do not delete inconvenient entries: doing so hides actual risk and distorts results.

Estimate expectancy from the average result of trades under the selected rules. After the practice series, compare actual entries, exits, costs and compliance. The sample’s average does not guarantee the same result in future. A live-account decision requires a separate risk assessment.

What to record for every trade:

  • Date and time: when the trade was opened.
  • Instrument: what you traded.
  • Direction: long or short.
  • Entry price: the price at which you opened.
  • Stop-loss: the protective order’s level.
  • Take-profit: the target.
  • Result: actual prices and quantity, profit or loss before and after costs.
  • Entry rationale: what you saw on the chart, such as a PCL, selling climax or level breakout.

If you cannot explain the entry rationale, mark the trade as a violation or accidental entry. Record the reason and the rule that needs to be restored.

For system analysis, separate rule-compliant trades from violations. Calculate the overall account result using every trade so an accidental loss does not disappear from the assessment.

Review the journal regularly: compare like conditions, costs, violations and losing streaks. A small successful group of trades does not prove an edge. If you change the rules, mark the new version separately.

Frequently asked questions

Is this trading course free?

 How long does it take to learn trading?

The time depends on your prior knowledge and practice time. Study the chapters in order, then complete and review the practice series. One hundred trades are the initial course assignment; there is no fixed timetable for moving to live trading or guaranteed readiness.

 What does a 1:3 risk/reward ratio mean?

 What is Smart Money in trading?

Smart Money is an approach to interpreting the activity of large market participants through price, volume and liquidity. The interpretation does not prove who was behind a particular bar or whether it was deliberate manipulation.

 Why can traders lose money?

Excessive position size, canceling a stop, unplanned additions to a loser and entering without checked conditions can cause losses. Record entry conditions and acceptable risk to check adherence to the plan.

Practice on demo first. A live trade requires enough funds for the instrument’s minimum size, collateral and acceptable loss. The same risk percentage is not safe for every account and system.

 What is a priority change level (PCL)?

A PCL is an area from which an impulse starts and makes a new selected extreme. In the course’s system, it is a structural reference: check a subsequent hold or break through the price reaction.

 How do you identify a false breakout?

A false breakout is a move beyond a marked level followed by a return. Higher volume or a candle pattern may accompany it but is not part of the required definition. Entry in this course requires a separate check of structure and risk.
