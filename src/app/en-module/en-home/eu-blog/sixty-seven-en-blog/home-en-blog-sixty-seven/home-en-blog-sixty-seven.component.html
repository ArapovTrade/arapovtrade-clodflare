1. How an exchange operates

Exchange vs broker

How is an exchange different from a broker?

An exchange organises trading between participants. A broker provides a client with access to markets and handles orders. They perform different roles.

Read the full explanation

Market participants

Who trades on financial markets?

Participants include individuals, banks and investment funds. Brokers, market makers and clearing organisations help provide access, liquidity and settlement.

Read the full explanation

Company shares

What is a share?

A share represents part ownership of a company. Its traded price changes as buyers and sellers agree on new transactions.

Read the full explanation

Exchange-traded assets

What can be traded on an exchange?

Exchanges can list shares, bonds and futures on currencies or commodities. Other markets operate outside a central exchange, so their data and trading arrangements differ.

Read the full explanation

Last traded price

What does the price on an exchange mean?

It is the price of the latest completed trade. New trades at different prices change that figure as buying and selling interact.

Read the full explanation

Order book basics

What is an order book?

It lists available buy and sell orders at different prices. Orders waiting at a price can be matched with orders seeking immediate execution.

Read the full explanation

Trade clearing

What is clearing?

Clearing organises the obligations created by a trade and helps manage the risk that a participant fails to pay or deliver. It is part of how an exchange market operates.

Read the full explanation

Busy trading hours

When does Arapov prefer to trade?

He favours periods with active trading, especially the overlap of London and US sessions. He prefers selected trades in active hours to watching a thin market all day.

Read the full explanation

Share dilution

What is share dilution?

If a company issues more shares, an existing holder owns a smaller percentage unless they buy more. Their share count may stay unchanged while the total grows.

Read the full explanation

Common vs preferred shares

How are common and preferred shares different?

Common shares generally provide voting rights. Preferred shares may have priority for dividends or other rights defined by their terms; the label alone does not guarantee payments.

2. How trades move prices

Supply and demand

Why do market prices move?

Prices change as buying and selling meet at different levels. Arapov compares price movement with volume to assess the balance between demand and supply.

Read the full explanation

Bid, ask and spread

What are bid, ask and spread?

Bid is the best available buying price; ask is the best available selling price. The gap between them is the spread.

Read the full explanation

Spread costs

Why does the spread matter for small trades?

The spread is a cost relative to the price move you seek. It can take a large share of a small scalping target even when it seems minor for a longer trade.

Read the full explanation

Liquidity and slippage

What happens when a market has low liquidity?

It may be harder to trade the desired size without moving price. Spreads can widen and the execution price can differ from the price expected.

Read the full explanation

Market volatility

What is volatility?

It describes how widely prices fluctuate, not whether they rise or fall. Arapov uses ATR as one guide to stop distance and position size.

Read the full explanation

Market maker

What does a market maker do?

A market maker provides buy and sell quotes and helps other participants trade. Its role is to provide liquidity, not necessarily to target an individual trader's stop.

Read the full explanation

Wash trading

What is wash trading?

It creates artificial activity, for example by trading with oneself. Reported volume may then give a misleading picture of independent buying and selling.

3. Placing and managing orders

Types of orders

What types of trading orders are there?

The basic types include market, limit and stop orders. They differ in how they balance execution speed, price control and activation conditions.

Read the full explanation

Market order

What is a market order?

It asks to trade at available prices as soon as possible. The final price can differ from the quote, especially for a large order or a fast-moving market.

Read the full explanation

Limit order

What is a limit order?

It sets the highest price you will pay or the lowest price you will accept. It controls the price condition, but the order may not fill.

Read the full explanation

Buy stop vs sell stop

How do buy stop and sell stop orders work?

A buy stop activates above the current price; a sell stop activates below it. After the trigger, execution follows the order type and venue rules.

Read the full explanation

Stop-limit vs stop-market

How is a stop-limit different from a stop-market?

A stop-limit activates a limit order, which may remain unfilled. A stop-market seeks execution at available prices, so its final price can differ from the trigger.

Read the full explanation

Protective stop-loss

Where does Arapov place a stop-loss?

He puts it beyond a meaningful price level with some room for fluctuations. He then sizes the position around the amount he is prepared to lose.

Read the full explanation

Stop hunting

Why can price move through obvious stop levels?

Many traders place stops near visible highs, lows or round numbers. Their activation creates trading activity; Arapov examines false breaks and returns around those areas.

Read the full explanation

Trailing protection

How does a trailing stop work?

It moves with a favourable price move to protect some profit. Arapov adjusts protection according to new price extremes rather than widening the loss in hope of recovery.

Read the full explanation

Iceberg order

What is an iceberg order?

It is a large order displayed in smaller portions while the rest stays hidden. Detailed order and trade data may reveal repeated activity at the same price.

Read the full explanation

Order splitting vs spoofing

How is splitting an order different from spoofing?

Splitting divides a real trade into smaller executions. Spoofing displays orders intended to mislead rather than trade. Arapov also checks completed-trade activity instead of trusting the displayed book alone.

Read the full explanation

Order duration

What do Day, GTC, IOC and FOK mean?

Day orders expire after the session; GTC orders remain under the venue's rules. IOC fills what is immediately available and cancels the rest; FOK requires an immediate full fill or cancellation.

Read the full explanation

OCO orders

What is an OCO order?

OCO links two orders so that filling one cancels the other. A stop-loss and take-profit pair is one example, with details depending on the platform.

Read the full explanation

4. Trading currencies

Forex basics

What is Forex?

Forex is the market for exchanging currencies. Prices are quoted in pairs, such as EUR/USD, showing one currency's value in another.

Read the full explanation

Forex participants

Who are the main participants in Forex?

Central banks, commercial banks, other institutions and individual traders participate. Their roles and trading sizes differ, so retail activity is only part of the market.

Read the full explanation

Forex vs stocks

How is Forex different from stock trading?

Forex is decentralised, while listed shares trade through exchanges. Currency trading commonly spans the working week, and no single Forex feed reports all global trading volume.

Read the full explanation

Long and short positions

How do long and short positions make money?

A long benefits from a rise; a short benefits from a fall. Plan the exit and loss limit before opening either position.

Read the full explanation

Forex swap

What is a Forex swap charge?

It is an overnight financing adjustment that can be a charge or credit. The terms depend on the pair and broker and can affect trades held for several days.

Read the full explanation

Leverage and margin call

What are leverage, a margin call and a stop-out?

Leverage gives exposure larger than the money put up, increasing both gains and losses. A margin call concerns insufficient collateral; a stop-out is forced closure under account rules.

Read the full explanation

Currency risk

What is currency risk?

It is the risk of a loss from an exchange-rate move. For a trader, leverage can magnify that loss, so Arapov uses modest exposure and planned stops.

Read the full explanation

Carry trade

What is a carry trade?

It seeks to earn the interest-rate difference between currencies. An unfavourable exchange-rate move can outweigh the interest received.

Read the full explanation

Forex trading sessions

Which Forex sessions does Arapov focus on?

He watches the overlap of London and New York for active trading in major pairs. The most useful window depends on the instrument and market activity.

Read the full explanation

US Dollar Index

What is DXY used for?

DXY tracks the dollar against a basket of currencies. Arapov uses it as background for currency analysis, while choosing entries on the pair's own chart.

Read the full explanation

Pips in Forex

What is a pip?

A pip is a conventional unit of currency-price movement, usually 0.0001 or 0.01 for yen pairs. Its cash value depends on the pair and position size.

Read the full explanation

Major, cross and exotic pairs

What types of currency pairs are there?

Major pairs include the US dollar; cross pairs do not. Exotic pairs include less widely traded currencies and may have higher trading costs.

Read the full explanation

5. Understanding economic events

Fundamental analysis

What is fundamental analysis?

It examines economic conditions, interest rates and company or market information. Arapov uses it as context rather than an exact entry timer.

Read the full explanation

Economic market drivers

What economic events can move markets?

Interest rates, inflation, employment, business activity, commodities and geopolitical events can influence prices. Their effect depends on expectations and the market situation.

Read the full explanation

Economic data surprises

Why does the same economic report cause different reactions?

Markets compare the release with what was expected. A strong-looking number can still disappoint if participants had expected more.

Read the full explanation

Central-bank interest rates

How can interest rates affect markets?

Rates influence borrowing costs, currencies and the attractiveness of assets. Arapov watches the market's response rather than assuming a fixed reaction to every decision.

Read the full explanation

Risk-on vs risk-off

What do risk-on and risk-off mean?

Risk-on describes greater willingness to hold risky assets. Risk-off describes a shift towards perceived safety. These are broad market conditions, not individual trade signals.

Read the full explanation

Economic calendar

What is an economic calendar?

It lists scheduled releases and events, often with forecasts and importance ratings. Arapov uses it to prepare for possible volatility, not as a buy or sell instruction.

Read the full explanation

Market expectations

Why do prices move before news is announced?

Participants trade on expectations as well as published facts. By the time news arrives, part of its possible effect may already be reflected in price.

Read the full explanation

Trading around news

Why does Arapov wait after major news?

The first reaction can involve sharp moves, wider spreads and reversals. He waits to assess price and volume around a meaningful level.

Read the full explanation

COT report

What does the COT report show?

It groups futures positions by participant category. Because it is published with a delay, Arapov uses it as positioning context rather than an immediate entry signal.

Read the full explanation

6. Gold, oil and stock indices

Gold trading

What is XAU/USD?

It is the price of gold in US dollars. Arapov examines levels and exchange-futures volume while keeping the risk of sharp moves in mind.

Read the full explanation

WTI vs Brent

How are WTI and Brent different?

They are different crude-oil benchmarks with their own futures contracts. Demand, supply decisions and geopolitical events can affect both and the gap between their prices.

Read the full explanation

Stock indices

What is a stock index?

It summarises a group of shares in one number. Traders can gain exposure through instruments such as index ETFs or futures rather than buying each share separately.

Read the full explanation

Trading different assets

Does Arapov use a different method for gold, oil and indices?

He applies the same basic ideas of levels, volume and price reactions across them. Position size and risk still need to reflect each instrument's volatility.

Read the full explanation

Oil inventory reports

Why do traders watch oil inventories?

Inventory data adds information about supply and demand. The reaction depends on expectations, so Arapov checks price and volume after the release.

Read the full explanation

7. Buying assets and trading contracts

Derivative contracts

What is a derivative?

It is a contract whose value depends on another asset or reference. Examples include futures, options, forwards and swaps.

Read the full explanation

Futures contract

What is a futures contract?

It is a standardised exchange contract with obligations defined by its terms. Settlement and clearing arrangements depend on the specific contract.

Read the full explanation

Spot vs futures

How is spot trading different from futures?

Spot involves buying or selling the asset itself. Futures involve a contract linked to it, with margin and settlement rules that can add leverage and forced-closure risk.

Read the full explanation

Futures margin risk

Does a small margin mean a small risk?

No. Gains and losses depend on the full position, not just the collateral deposited. A small deposit can therefore support a much larger exposure.

Read the full explanation

Learning spot first

Why does Arapov suggest learning spot before futures?

Unleveraged spot is simpler to understand because you hold the asset without the same margin structure. Futures require additional understanding of leverage, margin and liquidation.

Read the full explanation

Swap contracts

What is a swap contract?

It is an agreement to exchange payments under specified terms. Such contracts are generally negotiated outside an exchange and belong to the derivatives family.

Read the full explanation

Options vs futures

How is an option different from a future?

An option buyer pays for a right they do not have to exercise. A futures contract creates obligations under its terms, so the risks and settlement work differently.

Read the full explanation

Forward vs futures

How is a forward different from a future?

A forward has individually negotiated terms. Exchange-traded futures have standard specifications and exchange-based settlement arrangements.

Read the full explanation

Margin shortfall

What happens when a futures account runs short of margin?

It may need more collateral or a smaller position. Forced closure can occur under the broker's and exchange's rules.

Read the full explanation

8. Understanding debt investments

Bonds vs shares

How is a bond different from a share?

A bond represents a debt owed by an issuer under stated terms. A share represents ownership in a business. Their payment rights and risks differ.

Read the full explanation

Bond prices and rates

Why can bond prices fall when interest rates rise?

New bonds may offer more attractive returns, making an existing lower-paying bond less valuable. Bond prices and yields therefore tend to move in opposite directions.

Read the full explanation

Coupon vs bond yield

How is a coupon different from yield?

The coupon is the stated interest payment. Current yield compares it with the market price; yield to maturity also accounts for the price paid and repayment at maturity.

Read the full explanation

Bond risks

Can I lose money on a bond?

Yes. Risks include changes in rates and the issuer failing to pay. A high advertised yield may compensate for greater risk rather than offer a free advantage.

Read the full explanation

Bond yields and markets

Why do traders watch bond yields?

Yields provide context about interest rates and market expectations. Arapov uses them as a broader economic reference rather than a direct trade signal.

Read the full explanation

Bond duration

What does bond duration measure?

It helps estimate how sensitive a bond's price is to yield changes. Other things equal, higher duration means a larger price response.

Read the full explanation

Inverted yield curve

What is an inverted yield curve?

It occurs when shorter-term bonds yield more than longer-term ones. It is watched for economic context, but does not provide a precise timetable of future events.

Read the full explanation

Corporate bond spread

What is a corporate bond yield spread?

It is the yield gap between a corporate bond and a comparable government bond. A wider gap can reflect extra risk, not automatically a better investment.
