1. Reading a price chart

Technical analysis

What is technical analysis?

It studies price charts and trading volume to assess market movement. Traders use it to identify possible conditions for a trade.

Read the full explanation

Types of price charts

How are line, bar and candlestick charts different?

A line chart usually connects closing prices, although its input can be changed. Ordinary bars and candles also show the opening price and the highest and lowest prices for the period. Check the settings before comparing their shapes.

Read the full explanation

Bars vs candles

Do bars and candles show different data?

Ordinary bars and candles show the same opening, highest, lowest and closing prices for a period, with different drawings. Some bar settings omit the opening price. Synthetic charts can show calculated prices rather than the original recorded prices.

Read the full explanation

Chart timeframe

What is a timeframe?

It is the period covered by one candle or bar. On an hourly chart, each candle represents one hour of price movement.

Read the full explanation

Multiple timeframes

Why look at more than one timeframe?

A higher timeframe shows the broader movement. A lower one can help examine an entry more closely, keeping the trade in context.

Read the full explanation

Trend vs range

What is a trend, and what is a range?

A trend is a sustained move mainly up or down. A range is movement between boundaries without a clear lasting direction.

Read the full explanation

Support and resistance

Why treat support and resistance as zones?

Price may react near a previous level rather than exactly at it. Arapov therefore looks at an area of reactions instead of a single precise line.

Read the full explanation

Trading between levels

How does Arapov trade from one level to another?

He looks for an entry near a meaningful zone and a target near the next one. The plan includes where to exit if the idea fails.

Read the full explanation

Trend channel

What is a trend channel?

It is an area between two boundaries along which price moves. A channel can slope up, slope down or run sideways.

Read the full explanation

Impulse and pullback

How is a pullback different from an impulse?

An impulse advances the main move. A pullback temporarily moves in the opposite direction and gives back part of that move.

Read the full explanation

Chart pattern context

Why check levels before trading a pattern?

A shape alone does not explain the whole market situation. Arapov uses levels and volume to assess where the pattern formed and how price behaves there.

Read the full explanation

Volume on a chart

How does volume help with chart analysis?

Volume measures quantity traded in the selected data source. Compare that quantity with how far price moved and how it reacted near a level. More trading alone does not identify participants or establish the next direction.

2. When price crosses a level

Price breakout

What is a breakout?

It is a move beyond a support or resistance zone. Traders then check whether price stays beyond the boundary or quickly returns.

Read the full explanation

False breakout

How can I spot a false breakout?

Price moves beyond a level but returns inside the previous area. Arapov checks volume, whether price holds beyond the level and what happens next.

Read the full explanation

Stops beyond a level

Why can price break a level and quickly turn back?

Stops and other orders may sit beyond a visible level. When they trigger, they create trades, but that activity may not sustain further movement.

Read the full explanation

Breakout retest

What is a retest?

It is a return to a level after price has broken through it. The return helps show whether price behaves differently around that zone.

Read the full explanation

Breakout stop and target

Where does Arapov place a breakout stop and target?

In the failed downward-break purchase example, the low of that failed break helps define where the idea would be contradicted. An opposing price area provides a possible profit target. These references depend on the setup; a protective exit does not guarantee an exact fill.

Read the full explanation

3. Reading candle shapes

Japanese candlestick

What does a candlestick show?

It shows the open, close, high and low over a chosen period. The body spans the open and close; the wicks reach the highest and lowest prices.

Read the full explanation

Bullish vs bearish candle

What makes a candle bullish or bearish?

A bullish candle closes above its opening price. A bearish candle closes below it. Colours can be changed, so the prices matter more than the colour.

Read the full explanation

Pin bar

What is a pin bar?

A pin bar has a small body near one end, a dominant long wick at the other and a relatively short opposite wick. Price moved toward the long wick but returned before the candle closed. Its shape alone does not confirm a reversal.

Read the full explanation

Engulfing candle

What is an engulfing pattern?

It is a two-candle pattern in which the second body covers the first in the opposite direction. Its meaning depends on location and the next price move.

Read the full explanation

Hammer, doji and evening star

How are a hammer, doji and evening star different?

A hammer has a long lower wick; a doji closes near its opening price. An evening star is a three-candle pattern considered after a rise.

Read the full explanation

Candlestick confirmation

Is one candle enough to enter a trade?

The same candle can appear in very different conditions. Arapov also checks the level, volume and the following price reaction.

Read the full explanation

Harami pattern

What is a harami?

It is a two-candle pattern where the smaller candle's body sits within the previous larger body. It may show a pause, but does not confirm a reversal by itself.

Read the full explanation

Hanging man vs hammer

How is a hanging man different from a hammer?

Their shapes are similar: a small body with a long lower wick. A hammer is considered after a fall; a hanging man after a rise.

Read the full explanation

4. Recognising price patterns

Trading patterns

What is a chart pattern?

It is a recognisable price shape, such as a double top or triangle. A familiar shape does not guarantee a successful trade.

Read the full explanation

Reversal vs continuation

How do reversal and continuation patterns differ?

A reversal pattern points to a possible change in direction. A continuation pattern suggests a pause before the existing move resumes. Both need further checking.

Read the full explanation

Head and shoulders

How is a head-and-shoulders pattern confirmed?

A usual head-and-shoulders pattern has three peaks, with the middle peak higher. Its neckline joins the intervening lows; a break below that boundary completes the proposed reversal pattern. The shape and break do not guarantee further decline.

Read the full explanation

Double top and bottom

Do two peaks or lows always mean a reversal?

No. Price can remain in a range and continue the earlier move. Watch the level between the peaks or lows and how price behaves after it breaks.

Read the full explanation

Triangle patterns

What types of triangle patterns are there?

Ascending, descending and symmetrical triangles have narrowing boundaries. The shape alone does not guarantee which way price will break.

Read the full explanation

Flag vs pennant

How is a flag different from a pennant?

Both are pauses after a strong move. A flag has roughly parallel boundaries; a pennant has boundaries that converge.

Read the full explanation

Cup and handle

What is a cup-and-handle pattern?

Price forms a rounded decline and recovery resembling a cup, followed by a smaller pullback near its upper area called the handle. A break above the handle is the proposed completion condition. A familiar shape does not guarantee continuation.

Read the full explanation

1-2-3 reversal

What is a 1-2-3 pattern?

In a bullish example, point one is a low, two is the rebound high and three is a higher low. A break above two completes the proposed reversal. Entering at three is earlier and risks acting before that condition occurs; the bearish version reverses the directions.

Read the full explanation

Pattern entry signals

Is a chart shape enough to open a trade?

No. Arapov also checks the price level, volume, market reaction and stop location. Similar shapes can lead to different outcomes.

Read the full explanation

Rising and falling wedge

What is a wedge pattern?

A wedge has two narrowing boundaries sloping in the same direction. A rising or falling wedge may suggest weakening movement, but the breakout still needs checking.

Read the full explanation

5. Understanding wave counts

Elliott Wave basics

What is Elliott Wave Theory?

It labels directional movement and pullbacks as waves nested within larger waves. The basic teaching model has a five-wave phase followed by an A–B–C correction, but corrections can be more complex. A count is an interpretation, not a guaranteed future sequence.

Read the full explanation

Wave psychology

How do Elliott waves relate to trader emotions?

The theory interprets its patterns as changes in crowd confidence. A wave label does not measure any participant’s emotions, and a plausible count does not make the next movement certain.

Read the full explanation

Wave-counting mistakes

Why can people count Elliott waves differently?

Different starts, levels within the wave hierarchy and boundaries of an unfinished correction can produce different counts. Record those choices and the price condition that would invalidate the count before viewing later prices. Renumbering after the outcome does not prove the earlier count was useful.

Read the full explanation

6. Measuring pullbacks and targets

Fibonacci retracement

What do Fibonacci retracement levels show?

They mark fractions of one selected price movement. A 61.8% retracement gives back that fraction of the move, not necessarily 61.8% of the asset’s value. The marks are references to observe, not compulsory turning points.

Read the full explanation

Drawing Fibonacci levels

How do I draw a Fibonacci retracement?

Choose a clear price move and mark its start and end. Changing those points also changes the levels shown on the chart.

Read the full explanation

61.8% retracement

Does price always turn at 61.8%?

No. A 61.8% retracement describes the fraction of the selected move given back; it does not force a reversal or establish the strongest entry level.

Read the full explanation

Fibonacci trade setup

How does Arapov plan a Fibonacci trade?

The course example checks a price area, the response of price and trading activity. It does not specify a compulsory personal Fibonacci entry or stop rule. For a drawing, choose the move first, then set the price reaction and cancellation condition before testing it.

Read the full explanation

Fibonacci and volume

Why combine Fibonacci with volume?

Volume is the quantity traded in the selected source. Read it with the response of price near the chosen reference area: a large quantity alone does not confirm a reversal. The course teaches that relationship, not a separate compulsory Fibonacci setup.

Read the full explanation

Fibonacci extension

How is a Fibonacci extension different from a retracement?

A retracement measures the part of a selected move given back. An extension projects a chosen multiple of a move; in a three-point tool the projection starts at C. Depending on that start and multiplier, the projected price need not lie outside the original range.

Read the full explanation
