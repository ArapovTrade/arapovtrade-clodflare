Smart Money: what SMC means and how to use it

Smart Money is a term for large professional market participants and their capital. Smart Money Concepts, or SMC, is an approach to interpreting possible trading activity through price structure and marked areas on a chart. The useful task is to form a scenario, check the reaction when price reaches an area and decide whether the idea still holds. A marked area does not reveal which bank bought or where price must go next.

Who is meant by Smart Money?

Banks, investment funds and other professional participants can trade large positions and have advantages in infrastructure, execution speed and access to data. Size does not make them infallible. Large participants also lose money and take opposing positions.

Their objectives differ. A fund may buy as a long-term investment, a company may exchange currency to make a payment, and a market maker may quote bid and ask prices while managing its inventory. These trades are not all part of one coordinated plan against retail traders. Price develops through the interaction of orders serving different purposes.

In my approach, Smart Money provides a way to frame a question: where is trading activity high while price makes little progress? I compare the price area, volume and subsequent reaction. That helps assess a change in the balance between buying and selling without pretending to identify an individual participant.

Why large orders are split

A large purchase needs enough offers to sell. If little is available at the nearest price, a market order takes offers at progressively higher prices and the average purchase price becomes less favourable. The impact depends on the quantity available and how urgently the order must be completed.

An institution may split an order into smaller pieces and spread execution over time. An iceberg order displays only part of its total quantity, with more kept hidden. These methods help manage execution; they do not make every transaction invisible or guarantee a better outcome. Concealing an order's full size through a permitted order type is not inherently manipulation.

Order splitting explains why one chart bar, which records a period of trading, does not reveal an entire position. Several small trades may belong to one instruction, while high total trading volume may come from many participants. The market-microstructure guide explains how orders meet and what displayed and executed data can tell you.

Smart Money and the Wyckoff method

In the Wyckoff model, accumulation describes position-building, assessed through price and volume within a range. Distribution describes selling accumulated holdings to other participants. A directional move may follow, but sideways movement alone does not identify either process or establish the eventual direction.

I interpret SMC through Richard Wyckoff's connection between activity and price progress. If price leaves a range but the advance does not continue, I examine the buying activity and the following reaction. Wyckoff and SMC contain overlapping ideas; that does not make every label or rule identical. The Wyckoff-method guide explains the cycle and the development of accumulation and distribution ranges.

What liquidity means in SMC

Market liquidity means being able to trade the required quantity without a large deterioration in price. In SMC, liquidity also refers to suspected clusters of orders around highs, lows and other prominent areas. A cluster marked on a chart is an estimate, not a complete list of actual orders.

A protective stop is an instruction intended to close a position when its trigger condition is met. Above a high, there may be orders to buy a breakout and protective buy stops for sellers. Below a low, sell orders and buyers' protective sell stops may be triggered. A conventional stop that becomes a market order adds to transactions in the direction of the move. A stop-limit order works differently and may remain unfilled. The product, venue and broker determine the applicable rules.

In research on currency markets, Carol Osler found evidence that clustered stop orders can contribute to rapid, self-reinforcing price moves, called price cascades. This provides a mechanism for an accelerating move through an area. It does not establish that every such move was deliberately triggered or must reverse afterwards.

A large buyer needs sellers; a large seller needs buyers. Areas of substantial activity matter to execution as well as chart analysis. The liquidity-pools guide explains where SMC traders look for possible order clusters and how to check the price response.

Stop hunting and false breakouts

Stop hunting describes an interpretation that price was deliberately moved towards a cluster of protective orders. On a normal chart, the move is visible but its cause is not. News, incoming market orders, limited available quantity or a large participant's actions can all contribute. A brief move through a level does not establish deliberate targeting.

A false breakout occurs when price moves beyond a chosen boundary, fails to hold outside it and returns. A failed upward move is called a bull trap because it can draw in buyers; a failed downward move is called a bear trap because it can draw in sellers. At the moment of the initial breakout, the later result is unknown. Check the return and subsequent movement before calling it false.

In my analysis, a liquidity-grab idea needs that reaction. I examine movement after the return, trading activity near the area and changes in local highs or lows, not just a candle's long tail. High volume can occur in both a sustained breakout and a reversal. A simple rule that low volume always means a false breakout is not sufficient.

Illegal manipulation is a separate question. Spoofing involves orders intended to create a misleading impression of supply or demand rather than be executed. Assessing it requires information about orders and conduct, not just a price pattern. A false breakout is not proof of spoofing, and an iceberg order is not automatically illegal.

Order blocks, FVG, BOS and CHoCH

An order block, in a common SMC marking convention, is the area of the last opposite-direction candle before a strong move. Before an advance, for example, traders may mark the last falling candle. Different conventions choose its boundaries differently, so fix the rule before marking it. The area is not a record of confirmed bank orders. The order-block guide explains how to define and test such an area.

Imbalance means a mismatch between buying and selling interest, but SMC traders often use the term for a Fair Value Gap, or FVG. This marks a non-overlapping area between the first and third candles in a three-candle move. Transactions may have occurred there during the middle candle, so the shape does not prove an empty market or outstanding institutional orders. Price may revisit it, but need not. The imbalance-and-FVG guide explains the distinction and the marking rules.

A break of structure, or BOS, usually means price passes a selected previous high or low in the direction of the existing move. A change of character, or CHoCH, describes a break against that sequence. Before applying the labels, specify which highs and lows count and whether a candle must close beyond the boundary. Otherwise, different marking rules can give the same chart different labels.

These names describe price observations, not complete entry conditions. An order block may fail, an FVG may remain unfilled, and a CHoCH may be followed by renewed movement in the original direction. State in advance which reaction supports the scenario and which would cancel it.

What is ICT, and how does it differ from SMC?

ICT stands for Inner Circle Trader, the name under which Michael Huddleston publishes his educational material on reading price. In conversation, ICT can mean either the author or his approach. Here it refers to the approach: the observations, terminology and scenarios taught in his material.

SMC, or Smart Money Concepts, is a broader label for approaches to structure and presumed liquidity areas. It has no single mandatory vocabulary. Some terms overlap with ICT, but the label does not imply adherence to a particular Huddleston lesson. Identify the observable condition as well as its abbreviation.

ICT vocabulary describes hypotheses. It does not identify participants or establish that one algorithm controls price. For testing, record the swings selected in advance, when the condition became observable, and the subsequent outcome.

Key ICT concepts: range, displacement and market comparison

A range gives price a frame of reference; displacement describes movement; MSS identifies structural change; SMT identifies divergence between instruments. Combining these concepts does not yet produce an entry rule.

Dealing Range, Premium and Discount

A Dealing Range is the price interval selected for analysis between a significant low and high. Fix its boundaries and scale before assessing the current price location. Its midpoint is (high + low) / 2, called equilibrium in ICT terminology. The upper half is premium; the lower half is discount.

For boundaries of 100 and 120, the midpoint is 110: 115 is in premium and 105 in discount. This describes relative location within that particular range, not the fair value of the asset. A different range can produce a different classification. Premium does not require a sale, and discount does not require a purchase: price can continue moving within the same half.

Record the turning points defining the range and the event requiring a new selection. Do not move boundaries to fit an unsuccessful hypothesis.

BSL, SSL, ERL and IRL: identifying the reference points

BSL, Buy-Side Liquidity, denotes presumed buy orders above marked highs; SSL, Sell-Side Liquidity, denotes presumed sell orders below lows. These are chart reference points for possible orders, not visible confirmation of their quantity or ownership.

ERL, External Range Liquidity, refers to external targets beyond the selected dealing range. In specific ICT usage, IRL, Internal Range Liquidity, is associated with internal price inefficiencies, including FVGs. Do not automatically interpret IRL as all stops around internal swings. Define the range first, then distinguish an external swing from an internal zone. Selecting a different range changes the classification; a return to an internal zone is not guaranteed.

Displacement and MSS: movement and structural change

In ICT, displacement is pronounced directional price progress relative to surrounding candles. Look for rapid expansion and substantial bodies closing in the direction of the impulse; assess any FVG using its own conditions. One large candle, without reference to the surrounding structure, does not explain what has changed.

MSS, Market Structure Shift, is a local structural change associated in this model with displacement through a previously marked opposing swing. For a bullish case following a decline, test progress above a selected short-term high; for a bearish case following an advance, test progress below a selected low. The specific rule must state whether a touch is enough or a close beyond the level is required.

Do not automatically substitute BOS or CHoCH for MSS: authors use these labels differently. State the breached swing, movement and observation time. Zone definitions: order-block definition and testing; FVG construction and boundaries.

SMT: disagreement between related instruments

SMT stands for Smart Money Technique. Here it means comparing corresponding swings in related markets. For example, one of two instruments that usually move in the same direction makes a new low while the other stays above its previous low at a comparable time. The disagreement is between price structures; no oscillator is required.

Select the pair, timeframe, trading session and comparable moves beforehand. For instruments that usually move in opposite directions, compare opposite directions rather than mechanically comparing two lows. Trading hours, quote sources and the current relationship between markets can affect the comparison. SMT identifies disagreement; trade direction and invalidation must be specified separately.

Building an ICT scenario that can be tested

An educational bullish sequence can be written as follows: define the range and reference points → wait for a move below the marked low and a return → check for displacement above the selected local high → assess a possible return to a predefined zone.

Before revealing the continuation, record the entry criterion, invalidation event and maximum planned loss. If price never returns to the zone, the planned entry did not occur. If invalidation happens before confirmation, the original scenario has ended. A bearish version reverses the directions but still needs explicit conditions.

When comparing successful and unsuccessful outcomes, past candles, levels and volume must be identical before the branch. Include every case meeting the same rule and include execution costs. The result then reflects a reproducible sequence rather than an attractive explanation of a move already known.

Why add trading volume?

Trading volume records the quantity exchanged during a period, such as futures contracts. It helps compare activity with the result: how far did price move with substantial trading, and did it hold the level it reached? In my system, that check comes before an entry rather than being added to explain an already known reversal.

Heavy volume with little further progress is consistent with opposing orders limiting the move, but does not identify the buyer or seller. Every completed transaction has both sides. A rising candle does not prove that its entire volume belonged to one fund, and public order feeds can remain anonymous.

Compare the same instrument, venue and time interval. CME futures volume belongs to that contract, not all transactions in its underlying asset. Currency quote updates do not equal the quantity traded across global Forex markets. The volume-analysis guide explains data sources and how to compare activity with price.

How to check an entry scenario

In the course's working-with-levels example, I examine an upward move with active buying. The following rising bars become narrower, then a strong decline breaks local support areas. The learning task is to connect substantial activity, a lack of further progress and the subsequent change in movement. Buying solely on the strength of the first prominent bar misses that sequence.

My video on large-capital activity explains how I interpret the relationship between price, volume and position-building. It is an optional practical addition. The interpretation helps form a hypothesis; a trade still requires observable conditions rather than certainty that a particular large buyer is present.

Consider a hypothetical scenario in which the upper boundary of a range was marked before the next move. Price passes it with increased activity but fails to remain above it. After returning inside, the next decline breaks the nearest local low. This supports the interpretation of a failed upward breakout. If price instead regains and holds above the boundary, the rationale for a short position needs to be reassessed. No numerical return is implied by this example.

Before entering, define what would make the idea wrong and where the price structure provides a possible exit. A stop beyond the failed breakout's extreme is not protection against every later move or a guarantee of the execution price. If the required distance is too large, reduce the position or skip the trade rather than enlarge the allowed loss to accommodate an attractive zone. The risk-management guide explains position sizing.

For practice, mark areas before revealing the next candles. Record what you expected, which confirmation appeared and which movement cancelled the idea. Keep failed areas as well as successful returns. Moving a zone after seeing the outcome does not show whether the decision could have been made at the time.

Frequently asked questions

What does Smart Money mean in simple terms?

Smart Money refers to large professional participants and their capital. SMC interprets possible activity through price, but a marked area does not reveal a participant's identity or mean that larger traders always win.

 How do Smart Money participants differ from retail traders?

They can differ in capital, infrastructure and objectives. Banks, funds and market makers may take opposing positions. They should not be treated as one side that always knows the future.

 What is stop hunting?

It is an interpretation that price moved deliberately towards suspected protective exit orders. A chart shows the move, not the participant's intent. Triggered stops can reinforce movement without an inevitable reversal.

 How can I distinguish a false breakout?

Check whether price holds beyond the area and what happens next. A return inside indicates that the breakout has failed under that definition. A brief excursion or a volume figure alone is not a guaranteed trading signal.

 What can suggest possible Smart Money activity?

Compare activity with price progress and the reaction near an area. High volume records trading, but does not prove who carried it out or why. A later reaction helps test the interpretation.

 How can I avoid SMC traps?

Do not enter from a marked zone or a brief move through a level alone. Set the required confirmation, invalidation condition, position size and exit beforehand. A more distant protective exit is not inherently safer; the position must fit the chosen loss budget.

 How does ICT differ from SMC?

ICT is the approach associated with Michael Huddleston, known as the Inner Circle Trader. SMC is a broader label for methods of analysing structure and suspected liquidity; overlapping terminology does not make the approaches identical.
