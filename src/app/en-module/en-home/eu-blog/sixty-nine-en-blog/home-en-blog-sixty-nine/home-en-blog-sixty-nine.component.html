1. Getting started with crypto

Cryptocurrency basics

What is cryptocurrency?

It is a digital asset transferred through a blockchain. Its market value depends on demand, supply and the particular asset; a price rise is not guaranteed.

Read the full explanation

Blockchain transactions

What is a blockchain?

It is a shared record maintained across a network of computers. Check transfer details carefully: a mistaken crypto transfer is generally not reversed like a bank payment.

Read the full explanation

Bitcoin, altcoins and stablecoins

How are the main types of cryptocurrency different?

Bitcoin has a limited supply; Ethereum supports applications. Other cryptocurrencies are commonly called altcoins, while stablecoins aim to track a reference such as a currency.

Read the full explanation

Crypto market risks

How is crypto trading different from other markets?

Crypto can have large price swings, fragmented trading venues and varying protections. Arapov puts risk control ahead of the promise of easy access or fast gains.

Read the full explanation

Choosing a crypto exchange

What should I check before choosing a crypto exchange?

Look at reliability, regulation, liquidity, fees and withdrawal conditions. Arapov also separates long-term storage from the money needed for active trading.

Read the full explanation

First crypto trade

What should I plan before buying crypto?

Decide why you are entering, how much you can lose and where you will exit. Arapov favours a planned entry rather than rushing to buy a rising price.

Read the full explanation

Crypto chart analysis

How does Arapov analyse a crypto market?

He checks levels and volume along with supply factors, such as issuance and halvings. He uses measures like Bitcoin dominance as context rather than automatic signals.

Read the full explanation

Crypto demo trading

Why practise crypto trading on a demo account?

It lets you learn the platform and test rules without risking funds. Arapov recommends reviewing practice results before moving to a small live account.

Read the full explanation

KYC checks

What does KYC mean?

KYC means Know Your Customer: a service checks a customer's identity. Passing identity checks does not, by itself, prove that an exchange is reliable.

2. Keeping keys and funds secure

Hot vs cold wallet

How are hot, cold and exchange wallets different?

A hot wallet is connected online; cold storage keeps keys offline. With an exchange account, the service usually controls the keys rather than you.

Read the full explanation

Hardware wallet

What does a hardware wallet protect?

It keeps the private key inside a device rather than exposing it to the connected computer. You still need to check transaction details and protect the recovery phrase.

Read the full explanation

Seed phrase

Why should I keep my seed phrase offline?

The recovery words can give access to your funds. Photos, cloud copies and entering them into a fake website can expose them to someone else.

Read the full explanation

Wallet backup

How should I prepare a wallet backup?

Use a trusted device source, create your own recovery phrase and keep backups separately. Plan how to recover access without revealing the phrase to an untrusted service.

Read the full explanation

Exchange vs personal wallet

Should I keep all my crypto on an exchange?

Arapov keeps trading funds separate from longer-term storage. An exchange controls access to its service, while a personal wallet puts key management in your hands.

Read the full explanation

Authenticator vs SMS

Why can an authenticator be safer than SMS codes?

SMS depends on a phone number that can be hijacked through a SIM swap. An authenticator removes that particular dependency, though phishing and device risks remain.

English resource on this topic

Address poisoning

What is address poisoning?

An attacker places a lookalike address in transaction history, hoping you copy it. Check the full address against a trusted source rather than copying a familiar-looking entry.

English resource on this topic

Multisignature wallet

What is a multisignature wallet?

It requires several independent approvals to move funds. This can reduce reliance on one key, but the keys and recovery arrangements still need careful management.

Read the full explanation

3. Tokens linked to a reference price

Stablecoin peg

How does a stablecoin keep its price stable?

It uses a design intended to track a reference price, often one US dollar. Reserve-backed, crypto-backed and algorithmic designs maintain that link in different ways and carry different risks.

Read the full explanation

USDT vs USDC vs DAI

How are USDT, USDC and DAI different?

They differ in issuance, backing and control arrangements. The library compares liquidity, reporting and design rather than treating every dollar-linked token as identical.

Read the full explanation

Tether reserves

Why do USDT reserves matter?

The backing helps support confidence that tokens can be redeemed. Arapov examines the reserves and reporting rather than assuming a dollar peg makes the token risk-free.

Read the full explanation

Stablecoin control risks

How do centralised and decentralised stablecoin risks differ?

A central issuer may control or freeze transfers. A decentralised design has different dependencies, including smart contracts and collateral. Neither design removes all risk.

Read the full explanation

UST collapse

What did the UST collapse show?

The 2022 collapse showed that a promised dollar peg can fail. Arapov uses it to explain why stablecoin design and backing matter more than the word stable.

Read the full explanation

Synthetic stablecoin

What is a synthetic stablecoin?

It uses crypto positions and hedging to support its peg rather than relying only on bank reserves. Its stability depends on those positions and market conditions.

English resource on this topic

Frozen stablecoins

Can a stablecoin issuer freeze my tokens?

Some centralised tokens allow an issuer to freeze transfers at particular addresses. The possibility depends on the contract and the issuer's control rights.

Read the full explanation

USDT transfer network

Why must I choose the correct network when sending USDT?

The same token name can be used on different blockchains. The sender and receiving service must support the selected network, or funds may become inaccessible.

Read the full explanation

4. Crypto contracts and leverage

Perpetual futures

How is a perpetual contract different from a regular future?

A perpetual has no set expiry date. Funding payments help keep its price linked to the spot market, while regular futures have contract expiry terms.

Read the full explanation

Funding rate

What is a funding payment?

It is a payment between long and short perpetual positions, rather than simply an exchange fee. Its rate and timing depend on the contract and can affect holding costs.

Read the full explanation

Crypto leverage

How does leverage change gains and losses?

It gives a larger exposure than the collateral deposited. The same price move then has a larger effect on your collateral, in either direction.

Read the full explanation

Liquidation price

Why does higher leverage bring liquidation closer?

Less collateral supports the same market exposure, so a smaller adverse move can exhaust the required margin. Arapov plans risk and stops before that point.

Read the full explanation

Liquidation cascade

What is a liquidation cascade?

Forced closures add orders to a moving market. Those orders can push price further and trigger more closures, amplifying the move.

Read the full explanation

5. Financial services on a blockchain

DeFi basics

What is DeFi?

DeFi provides financial operations through blockchain smart contracts. A wallet can give access, but users also take responsibility for transactions and technical risks.

Read the full explanation

CeFi vs DeFi

How is CeFi different from DeFi?

CeFi relies on a company to manage the service and custody. DeFi uses blockchain applications and wallet interaction, changing who controls access and which risks you face.

Read the full explanation

DeFi building blocks

What services make up DeFi?

They include token exchanges, lending, stablecoins and data feeds called oracles. Connected services can pass a problem from one component to another.

Read the full explanation

DeFi swaps and pools

What can I do in DeFi?

Applications can offer token swaps, borrowing, lending and liquidity provision. High promised returns can involve high risks rather than a guaranteed income.

Read the full explanation

Smart-contract risk

Can an audited DeFi app still lose funds?

Yes. An audit can reduce some risks, but does not eliminate code errors or other dependencies. Arapov treats technical risk as part of the decision to use an app.

Read the full explanation

6. Supporting a Proof of Stake network

Crypto staking

What is staking?

In a Proof of Stake network, it commits coins to help support validation. Rewards come with network and asset risks rather than functioning like a guaranteed bank deposit.

Read the full explanation

Bitcoin vs Proof of Stake

Can Bitcoin be staked like Ethereum?

Bitcoin itself uses Proof of Work, not native Proof of Stake. Products described as Bitcoin staking involve other arrangements and should not be confused with its own consensus mechanism.

Read the full explanation

Validator vs delegation

How is running a validator different from delegating?

A validator operates network infrastructure. Delegation assigns stake to a validator rather than operating one yourself; the responsibilities and risks differ.

Read the full explanation

Unbonding and slashing

What are unbonding and slashing?

Unbonding is the process of leaving staking, which can involve a wait. Slashing is a penalty for certain validator failures. The rules depend on the network.

Read the full explanation

Staking risks

Can staking rewards be outweighed by losses?

Yes. The coin's price can fall more than the rewards earned. Access restrictions, validator problems and other service risks can also affect the result.

Read the full explanation

7. Understanding token supply

Tokenomics

What is tokenomics?

It is a token's economic design: supply, distribution, use and incentives. It helps explain where tokens come from and what may create demand for them.

Read the full explanation

Token supply and distribution

What should I check in a token's supply design?

Check issuance, who holds the tokens, release schedules, their use and the gap between circulating and fully diluted market value.

Read the full explanation

Token unlocks

What are vesting, cliffs and token unlocks?

Vesting is a release schedule; an unlock makes previously restricted tokens available. A cliff releases a batch at once, which can change the amount available to sell.

Read the full explanation

Token design examples

Why compare Bitcoin, ETH and LUNA tokenomics?

They illustrate different supply designs and demand sources. The library uses LUNA's collapse to show how a self-reinforcing issuance mechanism can fail.

Read the full explanation

Tokenomics warning signs

What tokenomics warning signs does Arapov watch for?

He checks concentrated holdings, unclear unlock schedules and weak reasons to use the token. A promotional story does not replace understanding its supply design.

Read the full explanation

8. Trading price differences

Crypto arbitrage

What is crypto arbitrage?

It tries to profit from a price difference across markets. Fees, speed and execution determine whether the apparent difference can become a real gain.

Read the full explanation

Exchange vs triangular arbitrage

How is triangular arbitrage different from exchange arbitrage?

Exchange arbitrage compares prices across venues. Triangular arbitrage moves through three trading pairs, usually without transferring funds to another exchange.

Read the full explanation

Other arbitrage strategies

Are there types of arbitrage besides exchange price gaps?

Yes. The library also discusses statistical, funding-rate and decentralised-exchange approaches. Each has its own data, execution and capital requirements.

Read the full explanation

P2P arbitrage

What risks come with P2P arbitrage?

It trades price differences in person-to-person markets. Counterparty problems, payment reversals or restrictions and account checks can outweigh the apparent margin.

Read the full explanation

Arbitrage costs

Why is arbitrage not risk-free profit?

Transfers take time, prices change and fees reduce the gap. Automation and infrastructure also matter, especially when competing for very small differences.

Read the full explanation

Delta-neutral position

What does delta-neutral mean?

Opposing positions can offset much of the exposure to price direction, such as spot buying with a comparable short perpetual. Funding, price differences and execution still create risk.

Read the full explanation

9. Recognising risks and scams

Types of crypto risk

What risks should a crypto beginner understand?

The library groups them into price risk, scams, technical security, regulatory changes and emotional decisions. Managing one category does not remove the others.

Read the full explanation

Crypto drawdowns

Can a cryptocurrency lose most of its value?

Yes. Deep falls are possible, and some smaller tokens may never recover. Arapov puts preserving capital ahead of chasing a lucky coin.

Read the full explanation

Crypto scams

What types of crypto scams should I recognise?

Examples include fake exchanges, phishing, empty projects and schemes promising guaranteed returns. The offer's credibility matters more than an impressive-looking website.

Read the full explanation

Pump and dump

How does a pump-and-dump scheme work?

Early holders build a position, promotion draws in buyers and the holders sell into that demand. Late buyers can be left with a falling price and little liquidity.

Read the full explanation

Scam warning signs

What should make me stop before sending funds?

Pressure to act quickly, an unclear team and requests for recovery words are warning signs. Guaranteed-profit promises also deserve close scrutiny.

Read the full explanation

10. Tokens driven by public attention

Memecoin basics

What is a memecoin in Arapov's approach?

He describes it as a token whose appeal relies mainly on memes, hype and public attention. He therefore treats its price as highly dependent on the crowd.

Read the full explanation

Memecoin liquidity

Why can a memecoin's price change so sharply?

Thin liquidity and concentrated holdings can let a large trade move price substantially. Promotion, panic and manipulative schemes can intensify that movement.

Read the full explanation

Memecoin supply and hype

How can supply and attention affect a memecoin?

Large token supply does not create lasting demand. In Arapov's explanation, fading attention can leave few buyers, regardless of the earlier hype.

Read the full explanation

Memecoin speculation

Why does Arapov compare memecoin speculation with a lottery?

He considers distorted activity and reliance on hype difficult to analyse reliably. His emphasis is the possibility of losing the full amount, not promised multiples.

Read the full explanation

Memecoin launchpad

What does a memecoin launchpad do?

It makes creating and initially distributing a token easier. Easy issuance can also produce projects with thin liquidity, concentrated holdings and little lasting value.

Read the full explanation

Bonding curve

What is a bonding curve?

It links a token's price with purchase or issuance parameters. In the model discussed, later purchases face a higher price, giving early and late buyers different entry conditions.

Read the full explanation
