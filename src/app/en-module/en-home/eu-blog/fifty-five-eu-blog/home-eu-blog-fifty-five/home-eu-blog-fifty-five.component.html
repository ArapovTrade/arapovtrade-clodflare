Candlestick patterns: how to read the shape and context

A candlestick shows four prices for a chosen period: the open, high, low and close. A candlestick pattern is one candle or a short sequence that traders examine for a possible change in movement. Reading it means checking both its shape and where it appears, then watching what price does next.

How a candlestick works

The body runs between the opening and closing prices. The upper wick extends from the top of the body to the high; the lower wick reaches from the bottom of the body to the low. A close above the open makes an ordinary candle bullish; a close below makes it bearish. An almost invisible body means those two prices were close, not that nothing happened during the period.

Green and red are common colours, but they are settings rather than fixed rules. Some chart options colour a candle by comparison with the previous close instead of its own open. Check which rule your chart uses. Read the actual prices before drawing a conclusion from colour alone.

An hourly candle groups an hour of activity; a daily candle groups the selected trading session. A long daily wick can contain several movements on a shorter chart. The chart-reading guide explains how the period changes the view without changing the underlying price history.

An unfinished candle is still changing. Its high, low and current closing value can move before the period ends, so an apparent pattern may disappear. A rule requiring a completed candle is checked at the close; entering earlier uses a different condition.

What the body and wicks show

A large body means a large difference between the open and close. Compare it with nearby bodies on the same timeframe, not its height on a resized screen. A small body can still have long wicks and a wide price range: a small open-to-close change does not necessarily mean quiet trading.

A long lower wick records prices below the body followed by a close above the low. An upper wick records the distance between the high and the upper edge of the body. Neither identifies who traded or proves that every seller or buyer has finished.

Those four recorded prices do not reveal the entire order of events. Different paths through the period can produce the same candle. If your decision depends on which extreme came first, examine more detailed data instead of inventing a story from the wick.

Pin bars, hammers and hanging men

A pin bar has a small body near one end of its range and a much longer wick at the other. The opposite wick is short or absent. Some recognition rules require the long wick to be at least twice the length of the body. That ratio defines a shape; it does not prove that the pattern makes money.

A lower-wick pin bar after a decline can be a reason to examine a possible upward reaction. An upper-wick pin bar after a rise can prompt a check for a downward reaction. The same shape in the middle of sideways movement has a different setting. Identify the preceding movement before choosing a direction.

A hammer has a small body near the top and a long lower wick after a fall. A hanging man has a similar shape after a rise. With a hammer, traders check for subsequent movement upward; with a hanging man, downward confirmation matters. The preceding movement distinguishes the names, not simply the body's colour.

A small body with a long upper wick after a rise is called a shooting star. A similar shape after a decline is an inverted hammer. A wick alone does not determine the next trade. The breakout guide explains the separate sequence of crossing a boundary, returning and testing the reaction.

Engulfing versus harami

An engulfing pattern uses two candles. In a bullish version, the second, bullish body extends beyond both ends of the first, bearish body. The bearish version reverses those directions. Compare bodies: the second candle does not have to cover both wicks of the first.

The location still matters. Bullish engulfing after a decline is not the same setup as the same two candles after an extended rise. The second large body records a substantial move from its open to its close. Continued movement after that close must still be checked.

A harami reverses the size arrangement: the second small body sits inside the earlier large body. An inside bar uses a different, full-range test. A harami's wicks can extend beyond the earlier body, so the two conditions are not interchangeable.

Doji, morning stars and evening stars

A doji has an open and close that are equal or very close. Judge that closeness against the instrument's price increments, the candle's range and nearby bodies; one fixed dollar difference does not fit every market. A doji records little net change without selecting the next direction. These patterns describe different things: a hammer is one candle with a small body near its high and a long lower wick after a decline; a doji compares its open and close; an evening star is a three-candle sequence after a rise. Their names alone do not confirm a reversal.

An evening star is examined after a rise. It has a large upward candle, a small middle candle and a bearish third candle closing well inside the first body. In the classical form, the middle body is separated above the first body by a gap. The bodies do not overlap, although their wicks can.

A morning star reverses the arrangement after a decline: a large downward candle, a small middle body separated below it and a bullish third candle closing into the first body. A common strict confirmation rule requires the third close beyond the first body's midpoint, upward for the morning star and downward for the evening star.

Markets trading almost continuously may not produce the classical gaps. A gapless three-candle sequence can show a similar pause and change, but it does not meet every condition of the strict pattern. Define any simplified version before testing it. Do not change the criteria after seeing the outcome.

Spinning tops and marubozu: body versus range

A spinning top has a small body and two shadows, each longer than its body. The strict marubozu example has no shadows: a rising candle opens at its low and closes at its high; a falling candle does the reverse. These names describe one period’s price geometry.

Compare the hypothetical candles. The spinning top has O = 105, C = 106, H = 110 and L = 100: a body of 1 within a range of 10. The rising marubozu has O = L = 100 and C = H = 108, so its body fills the range. Their shared colour does not make their shapes equivalent.

A small body does not imply low volatility; a large one does not guarantee follow-through. Compare completed neighbouring candles and the nearby level. Any tolerance for almost absent shadows should be specified before selection, in ticks for example, rather than treated as a profitability threshold.

Doji variants: long-legged, dragonfly and gravestone

A long-legged doji has near-equal opening and closing prices with substantial shadows on both sides. A dragonfly places those prices near the high with a long lower shadow. A gravestone places them near the low with a long upper shadow. Their names identify geometry rather than a completed trading decision.

The teaching diagram uses exact equality. The first doji opens and closes at 105 between a low of 100 and high of 110; the dragonfly at 110 with a low of 100; the gravestone at 100 with a high of 110. Specify any acceptable open-close difference before selecting real examples.

A dragonfly near support after a decline has a different context from the same shape inside a range. One candle cannot reveal the entire price path within the period or identify its participants. Wait for completion, mark the nearby level and state which subsequent reaction would support or invalidate the idea.

Inside and outside bars: compare the entire range

An inside bar has a lower high and higher low than the preceding bar. An outside bar has a higher high and lower low. This is the strict version; equal extremes require a separate rule chosen in advance.

Inside bars describe range contraction; outside bars describe range expansion. After a 100–110 range, an inside bar could span 101–109. An outside bar following 102–108 could span 100–110. Body colour and body size alone do not establish either classification.

Unlike harami and engulfing, these tests compare H and L rather than only O and C. An outside bar can have a small body that does not engulf the previous body. The eventual direction of an inside-bar break remains unknown. If testing a break of the mother bar, record its boundaries and the required closing condition before the subsequent candles appear. Do not move the boundary to accommodate the observed outcome.

Piercing line and dark cloud cover

After a decline, a piercing line pairs a long falling candle with a rising candle that opens below the previous low and closes above the first body’s midpoint. Dark cloud cover is the opposite case after an advance: the second candle opens above the previous high and closes below the first body’s midpoint.

To distinguish these examples from engulfing, the second close remains inside the first body. With a first open of 110 and close of 104, the midpoint is 107: a second close at 108 passes it without reaching 110. The opening gap is checked separately against the first low.

The required gap may be absent in continuously traded instruments. Record that as a different condition rather than calling every bounce the same classical pattern. Two completed candles establish the shape; whether the level holds and how price moves afterwards still need to be checked. A move through the opposing pattern extreme calls the reversal idea into question.

Tweezer tops and bottoms: repeated extremes

A tweezer bottom is assessed after a decline when two lows are nearly equal; a tweezer top follows an advance with nearly equal highs. In the selected two-candle version, the first candle follows the preceding direction and the second has the opposite colour. Repeated extreme prices are the feature to check.

Both hypothetical bottom lows equal 100; both top highs equal 107. Real extremes seldom match perfectly, so specify a tolerance before viewing the outcome, such as one tick for the chosen instrument. With a 0.25 tick, a 0.50 difference fails that particular rule.

Two adjacent touches alone do not establish durable support or resistance. Examine the preceding movement and later closes. A decisive move through the common extreme against the proposed reversal undermines the original idea. Do not widen the tolerance retrospectively merely to preserve the pattern name; use the same recorded rule across all examples.

Three white soldiers and three black crows

Three white soldiers are rising candles with substantial bodies after a decline. The second and third open inside the preceding body, with progressively higher closes near their highs. Three black crows are the corresponding falling sequence after an advance, with progressively lower closes near their lows.

In the rising example, closes are 104, 107 and 110; the later opens of 102 and 105 lie inside the preceding bodies. The falling example closes at 106, 103 and 100, with later opens of 108 and 105 also inside the preceding bodies. The numbers allow verification beyond simply counting three matching colours.

Colour alone is insufficient. By the third close, price may already have travelled a considerable distance towards a nearby opposing level. A completed shape does not make a late entry attractive. Compare body sizes with nearby completed candles and assess the distance to support or resistance; no fixed success rate follows from the name.

Rising and falling three methods: a pause in a trend

Rising three methods describe continuation within an advance: a long rising candle is followed by three smaller pullback candles and another long rising candle. This strict illustration keeps all three intervening high-low ranges within the first candle and requires the fifth close above the first high. Falling three methods reverse those conditions.

The first rising example spans 99–111, the three intervening candles stay inside those bounds and the last closes at 112. The falling example begins with the same 99–111 range but ends with a close at 98. Each panel includes its corresponding preceding movement.

Before the fifth candle this is only a possible pause. Without the required move beyond the first candle’s range, the pattern remains incomplete. If an intervening range leaves the original bounds, this strict example no longer applies. Do not confuse three methods with soldiers or crows: the former describe a pullback within an existing direction, while the latter assess a directional reversal sequence.

These sections cover the principal candlestick families. Rare variants with additional gaps and specialised algorithmic classifications require separate definitions; they are not prerequisites for reading the foundational patterns.

How to assess a candlestick pattern

Start with the preceding movement and the pattern's location. Support is an area where price previously turned upward; resistance is an area of downward reactions. Mark the area before the pattern appears. Drawing a convenient level afterward changes the test to fit the result.

Then compare the movement with trading volume, the shares or contracts traded during the period. Heavy activity with little progress differs from heavy activity followed by continued movement. Volume does not identify a participant or turn every candle into a reliable signal. The volume-analysis material explains how to compare these observations and check the data source.

In the course's trading-system example on Euro futures, a bullish pin bar appears on the pullback after resistance has been crossed. The test produces a higher low, followed by renewed upward movement, whose volume I also examine. I read the pin bar within that sequence. Its shape describes the local reaction; it does not replace the other conditions.

The video on using a pin bar shows how I relate the wick to the trend and level, including the planned exit if the idea fails. The written explanation remains sufficient without viewing it. Larger formations built from many candles have their own chart-patterns guide.

A candle at support: worked example

Consider a hypothetical share quoted in US dollars. Price had previously turned upward near $100. After another decline, an hourly candle opens at $101.50, records a low of $98 and a high of $102, then closes at $101.80. Its small body sits near the top of the range, with a much longer lower wick: its shape meets the criteria for a bullish pin bar.

The candle records a move below the earlier area and a close back above it. It does not tell you how many stops triggered or who bought. Decide in advance what further confirmation you require, such as another pullback holding the area followed by upward movement. Then check the next candles against that condition.

If a purchase is being considered after the check, the planned exit should match a failure of that idea, for example renewed movement below the observed $98 low. The closest possible stop is not automatically the best one. Size the position for the necessary exit distance and allow for the fact that actual stop execution can differ. Risk management explains that sizing process.

If later candles move below support and the decline continues, the expectation of a hold was not confirmed. The original pin bar recorded a return during one hour. It cannot override what happened afterwards or become proof of a reversal simply because the shape looked convincing.

How to practise without fitting the outcome

Practise with one pattern and write down its conditions before revealing subsequent candles. Record the period, preceding movement, location and required confirmation. If software marks patterns for you, inspect its recognition settings: different conditions can select different candles on the same chart.

Keep failed cases as well as favourable reactions. Assessing a trading rule also requires consistent entry and exit conditions, costs and all selected observations. A pattern name establishes neither a fixed probability of success nor a promised profit. The first useful test is whether you can apply the same definition without changing it when the result disappoints you.

Frequently asked questions

What does a candlestick show?

It shows the open, high, low and close for the chosen period. The body connects the open and close; the wicks reach the extremes. Those four prices do not reveal the entire sequence within the period.

 Which candlestick patterns should a beginner learn?

Pin bars, engulfing, harami, hammers, doji and stars offer a useful starting set. Learn how their shapes differ and where they are examined. The name alone does not supply a trade or a fixed probability of success.

 How is a pin bar different from engulfing?

A pin bar is one candle with a small body and long wick. Engulfing uses two candles, with the second body spanning the first in the opposite direction. Both need the preceding movement and location to interpret them.

 What does a doji show?

Its opening and closing prices are close. There may still have been wide movement between them. A doji does not tell you which direction the next candle will take.

 Can I enter on one candle alone?

In my approach, I check the level, preceding movement, volume and subsequent reaction. Before entering, I also identify what would invalidate the idea and where I would exit. One candle's shape does not replace that sequence.

 How is a harami different from an inside bar?

A harami requires the second body to sit inside the first body. An inside bar requires the second high and low to remain inside the previous full range. Check the right boundaries for the pattern you are testing.

 How does an inside bar differ from an outside bar?

An inside bar has a lower high and a higher low than the previous bar. An outside bar has a higher high and a lower low; the comparison uses the full range, including wicks, rather than candle bodies alone.
